cnn_decoder.py — local detail via convolution
Left: input patch. A 3×3 kernel slides over it. Right: feature response.

Convolution — the decoder's local craftsman

A tiny learnable kernel slides over the image, computing a weighted sum at each position. It is the core operation of every CNN and excels at local structure — edges, gradients, texture — exactly what places a crisp tumor boundary.

OncoSeg's decoder is CNN, not transformer, on purpose: after the Swin encoder decides what and roughly where, convolutions reconstruct the exact voxel edges. Training learns these kernel weights.

nonlinearity — ReLU · LeakyReLU · GELU · Sigmoid
f(x) curves. Without a nonlinearity, stacked layers collapse to one linear map.

Activation functions — where the network gets its power

Between linear layers sits a nonlinearity. ReLU = max(0,x) is cheap but can "die". LeakyReLU keeps a small negative slope (used in OncoSeg's decoder convs). GELU is the smooth gate inside the transformer FFN. Sigmoid squashes logits to (0,1) probabilities at the output head.

Each maps to a real spot in the code: LeakyReLU in cnn_decoder, GELU in the cross-attention FFN, sigmoid on the 3-channel output. Stack linear layers with no activation and you can only ever learn a straight line.

InstanceNorm3d / LayerNorm — stabilizing activations
Raw feature distribution (amber) → normalized to mean 0, var 1 (teal).

Normalization — keeping signals well-behaved

Deep nets train poorly when activations drift to huge or tiny scales. Normalization re-centers a group of values to mean 0, variance 1, then lets the network re-scale with learnable γ, β. The differences are which values get grouped.

OncoSeg uses InstanceNorm3d in the decoder (per-channel, per-sample — robust to the tiny batch sizes forced by 3D volumes) and LayerNorm inside the transformer blocks. BatchNorm, which averages across the batch, is fragile when batch=1 or 2.

ConvTranspose3d — learnable upsampling in the decoder
Each low-res cell paints a scaled copy of the kernel into the high-res grid; overlaps sum.

Transposed convolution — rebuilding resolution

The encoder shrank the volume 32×. To produce a full-resolution mask the decoder must grow it back. A transposed conv (a.k.a. "deconv") does the reverse of striding: every input cell is multiplied by a learnable kernel and stamped into a larger output, with overlaps added.

This is the ConvTranspose3d(stride=2) in every decoder stage, plus a final 4× head to undo the patch-embed downsample. Unlike fixed interpolation, the kernel is learned — so upsampling itself can sharpen edges.

swin_encoder.py — patch_size (4,4,4) tokenization
The volume is cut into non-overlapping patches; each becomes one token vector.

Patch embedding — turning a volume into tokens

A transformer eats a sequence, not a grid. Patch embedding slices the image into fixed p×p×p patches and linearly projects each into a embed_dim-vector "token". A 128³ volume with patch 4 becomes a 32³ grid of tokens.

This is the very first layer of the Swin encoder (patch_size=(4,4,4)). Bigger patches = fewer tokens = cheaper but coarser. It's why the encoder's finest feature map is at H/4, and why the decoder needs a 4× head to get back to voxels.

scaled_dot_product_attention.ipynb
Click a query token (left). Edge opacity = attention weight after softmax.

Self-Attention: every voxel asks every other voxel "how relevant are you?"

Each token emits a Query, a Key and a Value. Relevance between a query i and key j is their dot product qᵢ·kⱼ, scaled by 1/√d, then passed through softmax so the weights for one query sum to 1. The output is the softmax-weighted sum of Values.

Why it matters here: a CNN only sees a local neighborhood. Attention lets a voxel on one side of the brain directly reference tissue on the other — essential for deciding whether two bright blobs belong to one tumor.

Multi-head attention — many questions at once

Instead of one attention map, the embed_dim is split into H heads of size d/H. Each head computes its own Q·Kᵀ·V in a different subspace, so one head can track "bright edges" while another tracks "spatial proximity". Outputs are concatenated and projected.

OncoSeg scales heads with depth: num_heads=(3,6,12,24) across the four Swin stages. Watch how each head specializes on a different feature axis below — more heads = more parallel relationships, at fixed total compute.

swin_encoder.py — windowed + shifted attention
Grid = feature map. Colored squares = attention windows. Toggle the shift to see cross-window mixing.

Swin: attention that doesn't explode in cost

Global attention over a 128³ volume is impossible — cost grows as (number of voxels)². Swin restricts attention to small windows (here, square tiles), making cost linear in volume. To let information cross window borders, alternating layers shift the window grid by half a window.

Window-only attention would trap each region in its own tile forever. The shift is what stitches the whole volume together over successive layers — global reach, local cost.

oncoseg.py — encoder ↓ / bottleneck / decoder ↑ with skips
Resolution halves down the encoder, doubles up the decoder; skips bridge equal scales.

The U-Net shape — context down, detail up

Segmentation needs both global context (what is this?) and pixel precision (where exactly?). The U-Net solves this with a contracting encoder, an expanding decoder, and skip connections that hand high-resolution detail across the "U" so the decoder doesn't have to invent it.

OncoSeg's twist: the encoder is a Swin transformer, the decoder is CNN, and the skips are cross-attention (demo 09) instead of plain concatenation. Hover a stage to trace its skip. Channels 48→96→192→384 mirror back down.

Deep supervision — gradients reach every layer

Normally only the final output is scored. Deep supervision attaches a prediction head at each decoder stage and adds their losses (with decreasing weights), so even the deepest, coarsest layers get a direct training signal. This stabilizes training and helps tiny lesions survive downsampling.

Weights normalize to roughly [0.57, 0.29, 0.14] — finest scale counts most. Review caveat: the src library version forgets to interpolate, so its DS loss is shape-broken; only the inline trainer interpolates correctly. losses.py:79-90.

inference.py — sliding_window_inference over the full scan
An ROI-sized window tiles the full volume with overlap; predictions are blended.

Sliding-window inference — fitting a big scan through a small model

The model trains on 96–128³ ROI crops, but a real scan is 240×240×155. At test time a window of the ROI size slides across the volume with overlap; the overlapping predictions are averaged (Gaussian-weighted) so there are no seams at tile borders.

More overlap = smoother, slower. This is how OncoSeg processes a whole brain without ever holding it in memory at once. Raise the overlap and watch the seam artifacts vanish.

Class labels become 3 nested binary regions. Each channel has its own sigmoid.

The output head — multi-label, not multi-class

Brain-tumor regions nest: ET ⊂ TC ⊂ WT. So OncoSeg does not use a 4-way softmax. It derives 3 overlapping binary regions — Tumor Core (labels 2,3), Whole Tumor (1,2,3), Enhancing (3) — and predicts each with an independent sigmoid. The channels need not sum to 1.

This is the BraTS convention and it changes everything downstream: the loss is BCE+Dice per channel, and uncertainty must use binary entropy (demo 18) — a subtle bug-source the review flagged. Toggle a voxel's true class to see which channels fire. train_all.py:71-74.

losses.py — Dice + BCE, and why Focal handles imbalance
Per-voxel loss vs predicted prob for a positive voxel. Focal down-weights easy cases.

The training objective — Dice + cross-entropy (+ Focal)

Tumor voxels are a tiny minority, so plain cross-entropy lets the model "win" by predicting all-background. OncoSeg combines Dice loss (optimizes overlap directly, scale-invariant) with BCE (per-voxel calibration). Focal loss adds a (1−p)^γ factor that shrinks the loss on easy, confident voxels so training focuses on the hard boundary.

Drag γ: at γ=0 Focal = BCE; as γ grows, confidently-correct voxels contribute almost nothing, so gradient budget flows to the ambiguous tumor rim. This is the class-imbalance fix at the heart of medical segmentation.

metrics.py — Dice overlap & why it punishes small lesions
Teal = ground truth. Amber = prediction. Overlap = where both agree. Drag prediction.

Dice: the overlap score that drives everything

Dice = 2·|A∩B| / (|A|+|B|) — twice the overlap over the total mask size. 1.0 = perfect, 0 = no overlap. It's the training loss and the headline metric because plain pixel accuracy is useless when 99% of voxels are background.

Shrink the prediction and watch Dice collapse: for a small tumor, missing a handful of voxels costs far more Dice than the same miss on a large one. This is precisely why OncoSeg's worst cases are small-TC tumors — and why the review flags ET (often empty) as producing NaN Dice.

inference.py — Monte-Carlo Dropout uncertainty
Each pass randomly drops units → a slightly different mask. Spread across passes = uncertainty.

MC-Dropout: turning a network into an ensemble

Dropout normally only runs during training. Leave it on at inference and run the model N times: each pass silences a different random subset of units, giving N slightly different masks. The mean is the prediction; the per-voxel disagreement is the uncertainty.

Where passes agree (tumor center) → confident. Where they flicker (the boundary) → the model is honestly telling you "I'm not sure here" — the signal a radiologist would want highlighted.

entropy — binary (correct) vs categorical (the inference.py bug)
Entropy vs probability. Maximal at p=0.5 ("coin flip"), zero at 0 or 1.

Predictive entropy — quantifying "how unsure"

Once you have a probability, entropy turns it into a single uncertainty number. For a binary (sigmoid) output it's −p·log p − (1−p)·log(1−p) — peaking at p=0.5. The model is most uncertain at the decision boundary.

Review caveat made tangible: OncoSeg's output is sigmoid (independent channels), so binary entropy is correct. But inference.py uses the categorical formula −Σ p·log p, which omits the (1−p) mass and gives a different, invalid number. Drag p past 0.5 and compare the two curves.

uncertainty_qualitative_analysis.py — the ECE trap
Reliability diagram. Diagonal = perfect. Bars below = over-confident.

Calibration & ECE — and how background voxels hide a problem

Calibration asks: when the model says "90% sure", is it right 90% of the time? Expected Calibration Error (ECE) averages the gap |confidence − accuracy| across confidence bins, weighted by how many voxels fall in each bin.

This is the project's most important finding. Toggle the background mass: when 98% of voxels are easy background, the count-weighted ECE looks tiny (~0.01, "well-calibrated") — even though on the foreground the model is badly over-confident (foreground ECE ≈ 0.49). The headline metric averages the problem away.

metrics.py — boundary error: HD95 vs mean surface distance
Two contours. Lines = each boundary point's distance to the other surface.

Hausdorff HD95 — Dice can't see boundary spikes

Dice measures volume overlap but is blind to shape: a prediction can have great Dice yet a single nasty spur far from the tumor. Hausdorff distance measures the worst boundary disagreement; HD95 uses the 95th percentile to ignore lone outlier voxels, reporting error in millimetres.

This is OncoSeg's secondary metric (15.4mm vs UNet3D's 21.0mm — its most defensible result). Add a boundary spike and watch HD95 jump while Dice barely moves — exactly why both metrics are reported together.

Connected components — counting separate lesions

A segmentation mask is just "tumor / not tumor". To apply RECIST you must first split it into individual lesions — connected blobs of foreground voxels (6- or 26-connectivity in 3D). Each component is measured separately; tiny fragments below a size threshold are dropped as noise.

This is the bridge from a raw mask to clinical "target lesions". The diagnose-worst-case analysis counts these too: BRATS_077 had 31 fragmented WT components — a fragmented tumor is harder to segment and to measure. Raise the noise threshold to prune specks.

pretrain.py — DINO: emergent [CLS] attention, no labels
Patch grid = the input image. Amber dashed = the object. Teal = where the [CLS] token attends — diffuse early, locked onto the object late.

DINO: attention that finds the object — with zero labels

A Vision Transformer trained self-supervised (DINO: student matches a teacher across crops, no class labels) develops something striking: the [CLS] token's attention spontaneously localizes the salient object. Each patch gets an attention weight wi = softmax(β·featurei); we measure how peaked that map is with normalized entropy H = −Σ wi log wi / log N.

Drag training progress: at 0% features are random, so attention is uniform (H = 1.0) and the foreground gets only its fair area share (chance). As pretraining proceeds the inverse-temperature β grows, attention sharpens, entropy falls, and the foreground share races past chance toward ~100% — the object boundary emerges on its own.

Labels are the scarce resource in medical imaging — annotating a 3D tumor is hours of an expert's time. Self-supervised pretraining (DINO-style) is exactly how modern medical foundation models learn rich, object-aware features from oceans of unlabeled CT/MRI, so that fine-tuning a segmenter like OncoSeg needs only a handful of labeled scans. The entropy-drop / foreground-rise you see here is the signal that useful structure was learned before a single mask was ever shown.

mae_pretrain.py — mask patches, reconstruct the rest
Original → masked input (gray = hidden patches) → reconstruction. Drag the mask ratio.

Masked Autoencoder — learn by filling in the blanks

Cut the image into patches, hide most of them at random, and train the network to reconstruct the missing pixels from the few that remain. The encoder only ever sees the visible patches; a lightweight decoder paints the holes back in. No labels needed — the image is its own supervision.

The headline MAE finding (He et al., 2021): you can mask 75% of patches and still rebuild a plausible image, because natural scans are redundant — a handful of patches carry the gist. That redundancy is exactly why MAE-style self-supervised pretraining works so well for data-hungry 3D medical encoders: a model can learn anatomy from thousands of unlabelled volumes before ever seeing a hand-drawn tumor mask. Drag past 75% and watch the reconstruction stay structurally faithful — the tumor blob survives.

clip.py — contrastive image↔text cosine alignment
Teal = image embedding (drag the arc). Amber = caption embeddings. Bars = softmax(cosine).

CLIP: matching a picture to the words that describe it

CLIP projects an image and each candidate caption into the same vector space, normalized to unit length. Their agreement is the cosine similarity cos(θ) = img·text — which, for unit vectors, is just the dot product. A temperature-scaled softmax over those cosines turns the scores into a probability per caption, and the highest one is the prediction.

This single idea is the engine of zero-shot classification: to label a scan you never trained a classifier on, you embed it once and compare it to text prompts like "an MRI of an enhancing glioma" vs "a normal brain MRI" — no new head, no fine-tuning. The same image↔text bridge is what lets vision-language models draft a radiology report from pixels. Drag the image embedding around the arc and watch the winning caption flip.

diffusion.py — forward noising & the reverse score (denoise) direction
Left = noisy x_t (a brain slice + tumor blob). Right = clean target x₀. Amber arrows = the score: where the model pulls each pixel to remove noise.

Diffusion: corrupt with noise, then learn to walk back

A diffusion model defines a forward process that gradually drowns an image in Gaussian noise: x_t = √ᾱ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱ)·ε, where ᾱ(t) follows a cosine schedule from ≈1 (clean) at t=0 to ≈0 (pure noise) at t=1. A U-Net is trained to predict the noise ε at any t. Generation runs the process in reverse: estimate ε, subtract a step of it, repeat — climbing the score ∇ₓ log p(x_t) back onto the data manifold.

Diffusion is the SOTA engine for image synthesis and augmentation, and is now used as a segmentation prior: conditioning the reverse process can sample or sharpen plausible tumor masks, and the inherent stochasticity yields per-voxel uncertainty — the same signal the MC-Dropout widget estimates by a cruder route. Drag t: as noise rises the slice dissolves, σ grows, SNR drops, and the amber denoise arrows lengthen — that vector field is what the network learns to predict.

deepzoom — gigapixel WSI: resolution pyramid + tile reader
Stylized H&E thumbnail. The grid is the tiling at this pyramid level; one tile is what the GPU actually sees.

The whole-slide pyramid — when the image won't fit in memory

A digitized histology slide is a gigapixel image: scan a glass slide at 40× and you get on the order of 100,000 × 100,000 pixels ≈ 10¹⁰ pixels — about 30 GB raw. No GPU holds that. So the file is stored as a multi-resolution pyramid of fixed-size tiles (DeepZoom convention): level 0 is one coarse thumbnail tile, and each level up quadruples the resolution into 4ᴸ tiles (1, 4, 16, 64, 256, …) — higher level = finer detail = more tiles. You read only the tiles you need, at the zoom you need. (OpenSlide numbers it the other way: its level 0 is full-resolution.)

This is the opposite memory regime from OncoSeg. OncoSeg's MRI is a compact 128³ ≈ 2×10⁶-voxel volume that fits whole on one card — its challenge is 3D context. Histology can't fit at all, so the entire pipeline (tile → infer → stitch) is built around tiling, the same trick OncoSeg uses for inference (demo 13, sliding window) but forced on it from the very first byte.

color_deconvolution.py — H&E → Hematoxylin + Eosin (Ruifrok)
Left: raw H&E patch. Right: the channel(s) recovered by projecting onto the stain basis.

H&E stain deconvolution — unmixing two dyes

In histology, Hematoxylin stains nuclei blue-purple and Eosin stains stroma/cytoplasm pink. The camera only sees their mixture. Each dye absorbs light along a fixed colour direction, so in optical-density space OD = −log₁₀(I/I₀) the two stains form a basis. Inverting that 2-vector basis recovers each dye's concentration per pixel — a linear unmixing, exactly Ruifrok & Johnston (2001).

Stain colour drifts between labs, scanners and batches — the histology analog of MRI intensity drift in demo 03. Deconvolving onto canonical H/E vectors (then renormalizing) is stain normalization: it makes inputs comparable before they hit the network, just like InstanceNorm. Spin the basis angle to mis-set the vectors and watch the nuclei "bleed" into the stroma channel — a wrong basis corrupts every downstream feature.

Instance segmentation — counting cells, not just labelling tumor

Semantic segmentation answers "is this pixel a nucleus?" — so five touching nuclei come back as one fused blob. Instance segmentation must additionally say which nucleus each pixel belongs to. The classic recipe (and the spirit of HoVer-Net): take the foreground mask, compute its distance transform (each pixel's distance to the nearest edge), keep the high-distance peaks as markers, then run a watershed so every pixel joins its nearest marker.

Drag the threshold and watch the count vs the true 5. Too low → markers merge → the clump stays one object (under-split). The right band → exactly one marker per nucleus → correct count. Too high → the elongated dividing cell's distance ridge breaks into two peaks → over-split. OncoSeg outputs region masks (TC/WT/ET), not per-cell instances — so counting individual lesions or cells is a strictly harder problem layered on top, exactly the gap between a semantic mask and a clinical object count.

Top: 10 patches in one slide (amber = tumor). Bars = softmax attention. Bottom: pooled slide score vs 0.5 threshold.

Multiple-Instance Learning — one label for a whole bag

A gigapixel slide is far too big to label per pixel, but a pathologist can say "this slide is cancer / not". MIL learns from exactly that: the slide is a bag of N patch instances, and only the bag carries a label. The model scores each patch, computes an attention weight aᵢ = softmax(tanh(V·hᵢ) ⊙ sigmoid(U·hᵢ)), and pools them into one prediction z = Σ aᵢ·pᵢ (Ilse et al. 2018, ABMIL).

The MI assumption: a bag is positive if any instance is positive. Drag the slider — with zero tumor patches the slide reads benign; the first tumor patch flips it to malignant and grabs most of the attention. This is how you'd pre-train OncoSeg's encoder on slides/scans labeled only "cancer/not" — no voxel masks — and the attention bars double as a free interpretable heatmap of which patches drove the call.

foundation_model.py — frozen UNI/Virchow embeddings + kNN retrieval
30 H&E patches embedded in feature space. Click or drag to pick a query; lines = its k nearest neighbors. Retrieved class = majority vote.

Pathology foundation models — morphology becomes geometry

A pathology FM (UNI, Virchow, …) is a huge encoder pretrained on millions of unlabeled tissue patches. It maps each patch to a feature vector in which patches that look alike land near each other. Tumor, stroma and immune patches form distinct clusters — so a brand-new patch can be classified just by looking at who its neighbors are.

This is the modern recipe: freeze the foundation encoder and train only a tiny head (here, a parameter-free kNN vote). It is exactly the OncoSeg move — start from a pretrained encoder rather than learning vision from scratch on a few hundred scans. Frozen features that cluster cleanly are the whole game; the head can be almost trivial.

ViT: the [CLS] token, and why position must be injected

A Vision Transformer cuts the image into patch tokens, prepends one extra learnable [CLS] token, and lets attention run. The [CLS] token attends to every patch and becomes a single global summary vector. But attention has no built-in sense of where a patch was — so each patch token gets a positional encoding added before the first layer.

Without PE, [CLS] pooling is a pure set operation: Σ softmax(q·tokenₛ)·valueₛ is permutation-invariant, so a scrambled image and the real one produce the identical output (drag PE to 0 and shuffle — ‖Δ‖ stays exactly 0.000). PE breaks that symmetry by binding a position signature to each slot. This is the encoder family behind SAM, DINO and the medical foundation models OncoSeg's Swin transformer descends from.

positional_encoding — sin/cos at geometric frequencies
Top: the 24-d position code for token p (teal=sin, amber=cos, per frequency). Bottom: PE(p)·PE(p+Δ) vs offset Δ — invariant to where p sits.

Positional encoding — telling a transformer where a token is

Self-attention is permutation-blind: shuffle the patch tokens and the math is unchanged. So before attention, each token at index p is stamped with a fixed vector built from sines and cosines at geometrically spaced frequencies ω_k = 10000^(−2k/d): PE[p,2k]=sin(p·ω_k), PE[p,2k+1]=cos(p·ω_k). Low-k pairs spin fast (fine local position); high-k pairs spin slowly (coarse global position) — like the hands of a set of clocks.

The magic property: PE(p)·PE(p+Δ) = Σ_k cos(ω_k·Δ) — it depends only on the relative offset Δ, never on the absolute p. That is what lets attention learn "attend to my neighbour" once and apply it everywhere. In OncoSeg this is how the Swin/transformer encoder knows a voxel-patch's location in the 3D grid without convolution's hard-wired locality — and it's the exact idea RoPE generalises by rotating Q/K instead of adding the code.

moe_layer.py — sparse gate · top-k routing · load balance
Left: gate scores the token against 8 experts. Center: top-2 light up (run); the rest stay idle. Bottom: per-expert load over a token stream.

Mixture-of-Experts — capacity without the compute bill

A dense layer runs every parameter on every token. A sparse MoE layer holds N expert sub-networks but a tiny gate picks only the top-k per token: logits = Wᵍ·x, then softmax, then keep the k largest and renormalize their weights so they sum to 1. The output is that gated blend of just the k chosen experts — the other N−k do zero compute.

This is how Mixtral 8×7B and DeepSeek-V3 hit frontier quality cheaply: 8 experts of parameters, but only 2 run per token → ~25% of the FLOPs of using all of them. For a 3D medical model the same trick lets you grow specialist capacity (one expert good at edemas, another at enhancing cores) without paying for all of it on every voxel patch. The catch — watch the bottom bars — is load imbalance: a skewed token stream sends most tokens to a few experts, so MoE training adds an auxiliary load-balancing loss to spread tokens out.

state_space.py — selective scan hₜ = A·hₜ₋₁ + Bₜ·xₜ vs O(N²) attention
Top: the recurrent state carried left→right through the sequence, gated by content. Bottom: cost ∝ N² (attention) vs ∝ N (SSM).

State-space models — linear-time sequence modeling

Self-attention compares every token to every other token, so its cost grows as N². A state-space model (Mamba) instead carries a single recurrent hidden state: hₜ = A·hₜ₋₁ + Bₜ·xₜ. It walks the sequence once, updating that state token-by-token — a selective scan — so cost grows only as N, with O(1) memory per step.

What makes it selective: the input gate Bₜ = σ(w·xₜ) is content-dependent. The model decides per-token how much of the new input to write into memory, and A sets how long old information lingers. Drag A toward 1 and the state remembers longer; lower it and memory decays fast.

For OncoSeg-style problems this matters whenever the sequence is long: a 128³ volume flattens to ~2 million tokens, and a longitudinal study stacks many timepoints. Quadratic attention is hopeless there, but a linear-time scan stays tractable — the same reason Swin windows (demo 08) cap attention cost. SSMs are the emerging alternative to making long 3D/temporal medical sequences affordable.

Faded cells are never materialized. The amber box is the on-chip tile; only running max m and sum ℓ persist between tiles.

FlashAttention: same softmax, never store the N×N matrix

Plain attention computes the full score matrix S = QKᵀ/√d, writes all N² entries to HBM, runs softmax over each row, then multiplies by V. The bottleneck is not FLOPs — it is shuttling that N² matrix to and from slow GPU memory. FlashAttention streams S in column tiles and keeps each query row's softmax up to date with an online running max m and running sum ℓ, rescaling by exp(mₒₗ𝒹 − mₙₑ𝓌) as each tile arrives. The full row is never held at once.

Why it matters here: OncoSeg attends over large 3D volumes and across baseline/follow-up scans. The attention map alone would be tens of GB — impossible to materialize. Tiling makes peak memory grow with tile size, not with N², which is exactly what lets long-context and large-volume attention run on a real GPU. (Distinct from Swin: that shrinks the receptive field; this keeps full global attention but shrinks the memory/IO.)

World models: imagining the future, one frame at a time

A world model learns the dynamics of a scene: given the current state, predict the next frame. The same idea underlies any longitudinal medical prediction that rolls a model forward in time — predicting how a tumor evolves scan-to-scan: a 1-step forecast is trustworthy, but unrolling many steps without re-grounding on real data accumulates error fast. Dreamer: learn to act by imagining in a compact latent

A recurrent state-space model (RSSM) keeps a latent state h_t that evolves recurrently. From a handful of real observations it encodes a posterior latent — the coral squares o1…o4 landing on the data manifold. From the last real latent z₀ it then imagines a rollout entirely in latent space: the prior transition h_{t+1}=f(h_t,a_t) predicts the next latent and an actor picks each action, all without decoding a single pixel. Imagination is cheap precisely because it happens in a small latent, not in raw observations — rolling forward a 30-D state is orders of magnitude cheaper than rendering frames. nerf.py — render one pixel by ray marching the volume integral
Top: the density field σ(x) with the marching ray (dashed) and its sample weights (coral dots) → the rendered pixel swatch. Bottom: per-sample σ·δ (teal bars), transmittance Tᵢ (coral, decaying) and the running accumulated opacity (dashed, rising).

NeRF: turning a 3D field into one pixel by marching a ray

A Neural Radiance Field stores a scene as a function that, for any 3D point, returns a density σ and a view-dependent color c. To render a single pixel you shoot a ray from the eye and take N samples along it. Each slab of length δ absorbs light with opacity αᵢ = 1 − e−σᵢδ. How much of that slab's color actually reaches the eye is gated by the transmittance Tᵢ = e−Σj σⱼδ = Πj(1−αⱼ) — the fraction of light that survived everything in front of it.

The final pixel is the transmittance-weighted integral C = Σ Tᵢ·αᵢ·cᵢ, and the accumulated opacity Σ Tᵢ·αᵢ = 1 − TN. Drag the slider to sweep the ray across the scene: a ray through empty space stays transparent (T barely decays, opacity ≈ 0); a ray through the dense teal core saturates early (T collapses, weights pile up at the first surface it hits, color locks onto that blob); a ray grazing the deeper coral satellite blends warm. The weights peak exactly at the first opaque surface — that is why NeRF reconstructs sharp geometry rather than a foggy average.

This is the same accumulate-along-a-ray operator that defines CT: a CT projection integrates attenuation Σ σᵢδ through tissue, and reconstruction inverts many such rays into a 3D volume. NeRF generalizes it — learning a continuous σ/c field from 2D views and rendering it differentiably — which is precisely the machinery behind learned 3D reconstruction of an organ or lesion from sparse 2D slices/projections, and behind novel-view synthesis for surgical and interventional planning. The transmittance term is also what makes the render occlusion-aware: a deep structure can't contribute color the moment something solid sits in front of it.

rasterizer.cu — project 3D gaussians → 2D, sort by depth, α-composite
Left = composited novel view (rasterized). Right = the depth-sorted splat stack; front splats (small depth) are blended first and occlude those behind.

3D Gaussian Splatting — render a scene by stamping fuzzy blobs

Instead of an implicit MLP, the scene is an explicit cloud of 3D gaussians, each with a position, an anisotropic covariance (its 3D shape), a color and an opacity. To synthesize a new view you project every gaussian to a 2D elliptical footprint, sort them front-to-back by depth, and alpha-composite them onto the image — exactly the "over" operator: C = Σᵢ cᵢ·αᵢ·Tᵢ with transmittance Tᵢ = Πⱼ₌₁ⁱ⁻¹(1−αⱼ). Each pixel's splat weight is αᵢ = opacityᵢ · exp(−½·dᵀΣ⁻¹d) — the projected gaussian evaluated at that pixel.

Drag splats rendered: the renderer adds gaussians in depth order. Early (front) splats lay down color and spend transmittance; once T drops near zero the surface is opaque and farther splats are culled — the same early-termination a real tile rasterizer uses.

The contrast with NeRF (demo on diffusion/JEPA-style implicit models) is the whole point: NeRF answers each pixel by marching a ray and evaluating a per-ray MLP at dozens of sample points — accurate but slow. Gaussian splatting is a single rasterize pass (sort + blend), no network in the loop, so it renders novel views at 100+ FPS. For medical/clinical use that means a 3D capture of a patient's anatomy or a surgical field can be re-viewed interactively in real time, and the explicit gaussians are directly editable (delete, recolor, deform a structure) in a way an opaque MLP volume is not.

pointnet.py — set input → shared MLP → max-pool → permutation-invariant feature

PointNet: learning on a SET, not a sequence

A point cloud is an unordered set — there is no canonical "point #0". PointNet handles this with three steps: (1) a shared per-point MLP f(pᵢ) applied independently with the same weights to every point, (2) a symmetric pool — element-wise max over all points, (3) the resulting global vector g[d] = maxᵢ f(pᵢ)[d]. Because max over a set ignores order, g is permutation-invariant by construction: g(π·X) = g(X) for any reordering π.

Step the order control through 6 permutations of the same 8 points. The position labels on the left scramble, the feature columns in the middle slide around — but the global vector g on the right is byte-identical every time, and the physical point that supplies each dimension's max (ink-outlined) is unchanged. Tie to OncoSeg: dense CT/MRI voxels do have a natural grid order, so OncoSeg uses ordered conv + windowed attention. But the instant you work with sparse 3D — point-sampled lesion surfaces, centroid sets, multi-detector fusion, or LiDAR-style sensing with no natural order — you need set-based, permutation-invariant operators. Max-pool is the simplest faithful one.

Sim-to-real domain randomization

A model only generalizes to inputs it was effectively trained on. Plot every image as a point in an abstract appearance space (x = texture/contrast, y = brightness/illumination). The training distribution is a cloud of synthetic samples whose appearance we deliberately randomize; its spread is the randomization strength σ. One fixed real sample (coral ◆) sits off to the side — the reality gap.

At small σ the real sample is far outside the cloud (out-of-distribution): the model never saw anything like it, so it fails. Crank σ and the cloud expands; once the support contour (drawn at 2.5σ) encloses the real point, it becomes in-distribution — the real domain is now just one more sample the network already trained against.

In OncoSeg terms this is the deployment analog of augmenting across MRI/CT scanners and sites. A tumor model trained on one hospital's scanner is out-of-distribution on the next (different bias field, contrast, noise). Randomizing intensity/texture/orientation during training widens the appearance cloud so an unseen scanner lands inside it — bridging the reality gap without collecting labels from every new site. Over-randomize, though, and you waste capacity covering appearances no scanner ever produces.

diffusion_policy.py — denoise an ACTION trajectory, conditioned on the observation
Faint teal dashes = clean target x₀. Solid line = the path at denoise step k (coral→teal as it cleans). Coral stubs = the per-waypoint denoise pull toward x₀. Bar = noise σ.

Diffusion Policy — generate the plan by denoising it

Instead of emitting one action at a time, a diffusion policy treats the next H-step action trajectory as a single object to generate. It starts from a path of pure Gaussian noise and runs a learned reverse diffusion: at each step k (counting T→0) a network predicts the noise and subtracts a slice of it, so the random scribble collapses into a smooth, feasible path that reaches the goal and arcs around the obstacle — all conditioned on the observation.

Each waypoint follows the DDPM relation xₖ = √ᾱₖ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱₖ)·ε with a cosine schedule for ᾱ. Drag k from T down to 0: the signal weight √ᾱ rises, the noise level σ=√(1−ᾱ) falls, the signal-to-noise ratio climbs, and the trajectory snaps onto the clean target. The coral stubs are the denoise direction the model would apply next.

This is SOTA visuomotor control (Chi et al., 2023; π0). It is deliberately distinct from the VLA widget: VLA emits discrete, tokenized actions one bin at a time, whereas a diffusion policy produces a continuous, multimodal whole-trajectory distribution — switch the goal and it commits to a different smooth mode rather than averaging them. The same denoise-a-structured-output recipe is why diffusion is creeping into medical imaging as a segmentation / registration prior: sample plausible masks or deformations rather than regress one blurry mean — and for surgical-robot or auto-contouring deployment, generating a full feasible trajectory beats greedy per-step decoding.

pinn.py - damped oscillator
Blue=true physical u(t), teal=network fit, coral dots=sparse data, coral shading=local |ODE residual|.

Physics-Informed NN (PINN)

Fit a curve u(t) through a handful of noisy measurements of a damped harmonic oscillator. With only the data term, infinitely many wiggly curves pass near the points — the network happily picks one and overfits between samples. A PINN adds a second loss: the residual of the governing equation r(t) = u″ + 2ζω·u′ + ω²·u, which must be ~0 if u truly obeys the physics. Slide λ to weight that residual. At λ=0 the fit oscillates and r(t) is huge; as λ→1 the only curve that satisfies both the data and the equation is the smooth physical solution.

Total loss = Ldata + λ·Lresidual. The residual is evaluated everywhere via autodiff (here: finite differences), not just at labeled points — so the equation regularizes the gaps where labels are missing. In OncoSeg-style settings with scarce annotations, encoding known biology/physics (smooth boundary growth, conservation, dose kinetics) as a soft constraint stops the model from producing impossible predictions and improves generalization from tiny cohorts.

quantize.py - PTQ FP32 -> INT8
Teal grid = 2^b reconstruction levels. Bars = weight histogram, colored by |rounding error| (coral = large). Blue = accuracy vs bit-width.

Quantization: FP32 -> INT8

A trained net stores each weight as a 32-bit float. Post-training quantization snaps those continuous values onto a small grid of integers. With a per-tensor scale s and symmetric mapping, each weight becomes w_q = round(w / s), an integer in [-2^(b-1), 2^(b-1)-1], and is read back as w_q · s. The choice s = max|w| / (2^(b-1)-1) spends the whole integer range on the observed weights. A b-bit code has only 2^b levels, so the grid spacing — and the rounding error — doubles for every bit you drop.

Why it matters for OncoSeg: a 3D segmentation backbone is large, but hospitals rarely have datacenter GPUs at the bedside. INT8 makes the weights 4× smaller (8/32 of FP32) and lets integer matmul run on a clinical workstation or edge appliance with near-identical Dice. Push too far, though — the accuracy strip shows INT8 and INT6 sit on a plateau, but at 2-bit the rounding noise swamps the signal and validation accuracy falls off a cliff. The engineering job is finding the lowest bit-width before that knee.

Magnitude pruning & sparsity

A trained network is mostly dead weight: many connections have a tiny magnitude and barely move the output. Unstructured magnitude pruning ranks every weight by |w| and zeros out the smallest ones until you hit a target sparsity (% of weights set to 0). The surviving weights are untouched. Slide the target up and watch the smallest-magnitude cells drop to grey (zeroed) as the coral threshold sweeps right across the |w| color bar.

For a hospital deployment, this is how you ship a segmentation model that fits on the edge box next to the scanner. You can usually delete 70–80% of the weights and lose almost nothing on the validation set — the accuracy curve is flat. Push past the knee (~85–90%) and it falls off a cliff: you have started cutting load-bearing weights, not just dead ones. The sweet spot is the largest sparsity still on the plateau — a smaller, faster model with no clinically meaningful drop. (Sparse weights also store and multiply faster — only the nonzeros cost anything.)

distill.py — soften teacher logits, train the student to match
Blue = teacher logits (fixed). Teal bars = soft targets at τ; coral = the runner-up "dark-knowledge" class. Dashed line = the one-hot hard label.

Knowledge distillation — train a small student on a big teacher's soft labels

A large teacher outputs logits over the classes. Instead of training a small student on the one-hot ground-truth label, distillation trains it to match the teacher's softened probabilities p = softmax(z / τ). A temperature τ > 1 flattens the distribution so the small probabilities the teacher assigns to the wrong classes become visible — and copyable.

Those small probabilities are "dark knowledge": the teacher quietly says this enhancing glioma also looks a bit like a meningioma or a post-op cavity, and nothing like a normal brain. The hard argmax keeps only the winner (entropy = 0); the soft target carries this whole similarity structure, so the student learns far more per example.

For deployment this is the compression lever: a 100M-parameter teacher can hand its behaviour to a student small enough to run on the scanner console or a single on-prem GPU — keeping accuracy while shrinking memory and latency at the point of care. It pairs naturally with pruning and quantization, and with self-supervised pretraining (DINO/MAE) which is how the teacher learned cheaply in the first place. The distillation loss is the KL between teacher and student soft targets, scaled by τ² to keep gradient magnitudes stable.

finetune.py — LoRA: freeze W, learn only ΔW = B·A
Faint slate = frozen W (d×d). Teal = B (d×r), coral = A (r×d) — the only trainable weights. Bottom-right = their product ΔW = B·A, a genuine rank-r matrix. Drag the rank.

LoRA — fine-tune a giant model by training two thin matrices

A pretrained weight matrix W is d×d. Full fine-tuning re-trains all d² of its entries. LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) freezes W entirely and learns only a low-rank update ΔW = B·A, where B is d×r and A is r×d with rank r ≪ d. At inference the model uses W + B·A. The product of a (d×r) and an (r×d) matrix can have rank at most r, so ΔW is forced to be low-rank — and that is the whole point.

Drag rank r. Trainable parameters are 2·d·r instead of d²; the bar tracks how much of the ideal full update ΔW the rank-r factorization captures. Because real weight updates have a steeply decaying spectrum, even a tiny r recovers almost all of it — r=4 already captures >98% here.

This is the modern recipe for adapting a frozen medical foundation model to a new task on a single GPU. A 3D CT/MRI encoder may carry hundreds of millions of pretrained weights; retraining them for a new tumor type risks catastrophic forgetting and demands huge compute and data. LoRA instead bolts a handful of thin B/A factors onto each attention/projection layer — at d=4096 that is >99% fewer trainable parameters — so a hospital can specialize a shared foundation model to its own scanner and protocol from a small labeled set, ship just the megabytes-sized adapters, and keep the original weights untouched.

Mixed precision & loss scaling

Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) does the heavy matmuls in FP16 — half the memory, ~2× the throughput on tensor cores — while keeping an FP32 master copy of the weights for the actual update. The catch: FP16 can only represent magnitudes down to its smallest subnormal, 2-24 ≈ 6e-8. Any gradient smaller than that flushes to exactly 0 and that learning signal is lost forever.

Loss scaling rescues them. Multiply the loss by a large factor S (a power of two) before backward(); by the chain rule every gradient is scaled by the same S, shifting the whole cloud up into FP16's representable band. Right before the FP32 master-weight update you divide the gradients back by S — so the math is unchanged, you've only borrowed dynamic range. Drag the slider: watch the sub-floor gradients climb out of the underflow zone. Push S too far and the largest gradient hits the 65504 ceiling → Inf; a real dynamic loss scaler detects that, halves S and skips the step.

Per the OncoSeg training review, the notebook trained in pure FP32 and was the exact memory/throughput win it was missing. A 3D multi-scale tumor volume is enormous; AMP roughly halves activation memory, letting you fit a larger patch or batch (better BatchNorm/Dice statistics) and train faster on the same hospital GPU. Dice-style losses on tiny lesions produce very small gradients — precisely the regime where loss scaling prevents silent underflow that would otherwise stall learning on the smallest, most clinically important tumors.

cell_graph_gnn.py — k-NN message passing
Coral = tumor nuclei, teal = immune nuclei. The ringed node is the cell being updated; lines = its k nearest-neighbor edges; its inner core recolors to the aggregated feature.

Cell-graph GNN

Instead of one image patch, model the tissue as a graph: each detected nucleus is a node carrying a feature (here a scalar “tumor-ness” score), and edges connect each node to its k nearest spatial neighbors. A single GNN message-passing step replaces a node’s feature with the mean of its k neighbors’ features — a mean-pool aggregation — the core idea behind GCN & GraphSAGE-mean (which additionally keep the node’s own feature and apply a learned weight + nonlinearity; dropped here for clarity). Slide k to grow the neighborhood and watch the highlighted tumor cell’s feature get pulled toward its surroundings.

The highlighted cell is a tumor nucleus (original feature 0.85) but it sits inside a ring of immune cells. With small k, all neighbors are immune, so message passing rewrites its feature down to ~0.16 — the model now “knows” this tumor cell is immune-infiltrated. This tumor-microenvironment context is invisible to a per-patch CNN cropped on that single nucleus; it only emerges from the spatial graph. Raise k past the immune ring (k≥7) and the nearby tumor cells re-enter, nudging the feature back up — a direct view of how neighborhood size sets the receptive field of a cell-graph model.

Macenko stain normalization — beating the scanner colour cast

Send one glass slide to two labs and the H&E images come back different colours: scanner optics, dye lots and protocols give every site its own cast. A model trained on lab-A pinks then sees lab-B purples as a brand-new distribution. Macenko et al. (2009) fixes this without touching the network: work in optical density OD = −log₁₀(I/I₀), estimate each image's two-vector stain basis Mₛ = {H,E}, deconvolve to per-stain concentrations C = Mₛ⁻¹·OD, rescale those concentrations so their robust (99th-percentile) maximum matches a fixed reference, then recompose in the reference basis M_ref. The output wears the reference's colours but keeps the source's tissue structure.

This is a domain-shift fix, the histology twin of MRI intensity normalization in demo 03. Drag the cast: the raw SOURCE marches away from the REFERENCE (red ΔE bar climbs), yet the NORMALIZED output stays pinned at ΔE≈0 (teal bar) for every cast — because by construction it is the reference's stain matrix at the reference's concentration scale. Run this as a deterministic pre-processing step and a hospital can deploy one model across every scanner it owns.
