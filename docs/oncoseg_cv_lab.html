Convolution — the decoder's local craftsman

A tiny learnable kernel slides over the image, computing a weighted sum at each position. It is the core operation of every CNN and excels at local structure — edges, gradients, texture — exactly what places a crisp tumor boundary.

OncoSeg's decoder is CNN, not transformer, on purpose: after the Swin encoder decides what and roughly where, convolutions reconstruct the exact voxel edges. Training learns these kernel weights.

Activation functions — where the network gets its power

Between linear layers sits a nonlinearity. ReLU = max(0,x) is cheap but can "die". LeakyReLU keeps a small negative slope (used in OncoSeg's decoder convs). GELU is the smooth gate inside the transformer FFN. Sigmoid squashes logits to (0,1) probabilities at the output head.

Each maps to a real spot in the code: LeakyReLU in cnn_decoder, GELU in the cross-attention FFN, sigmoid on the 3-channel output. Stack linear layers with no activation and you can only ever learn a straight line.

InstanceNorm3d / LayerNorm — stabilizing activations
Raw feature distribution (amber) → normalized to mean 0, var 1 (teal).

Normalization — keeping signals well-behaved

Deep nets train poorly when activations drift to huge or tiny scales. Normalization re-centers a group of values to mean 0, variance 1, then lets the network re-scale with learnable γ, β. The differences are which values get grouped.

OncoSeg uses InstanceNorm3d in the decoder (per-channel, per-sample — robust to the tiny batch sizes forced by 3D volumes) and LayerNorm inside the transformer blocks. BatchNorm, which averages across the batch, is fragile when batch=1 or 2.

ConvTranspose3d — learnable upsampling in the decoder
Each low-res cell paints a scaled copy of the kernel into the high-res grid; overlaps sum.

Transposed convolution — rebuilding resolution

The encoder shrank the volume 32×. To produce a full-resolution mask the decoder must grow it back. A transposed conv (a.k.a. "deconv") does the reverse of striding: every input cell is multiplied by a learnable kernel and stamped into a larger output, with overlaps added.

This is the ConvTranspose3d(stride=2) in every decoder stage, plus a final 4× head to undo the patch-embed downsample. Unlike fixed interpolation, the kernel is learned — so upsampling itself can sharpen edges.

swin_encoder.py — patch_size (4,4,4) tokenization
The volume is cut into non-overlapping patches; each becomes one token vector.

Patch embedding — turning a volume into tokens

A transformer eats a sequence, not a grid. Patch embedding slices the image into fixed p×p×p patches and linearly projects each into a embed_dim-vector "token". A 128³ volume with patch 4 becomes a 32³ grid of tokens.

This is the very first layer of the Swin encoder (patch_size=(4,4,4)). Bigger patches = fewer tokens = cheaper but coarser. It's why the encoder's finest feature map is at H/4, and why the decoder needs a 4× head to get back to voxels.

Self-Attention: every voxel asks every other voxel "how relevant are you?"

Each token emits a Query, a Key and a Value. Relevance between a query i and key j is their dot product qᵢ·kⱼ, scaled by 1/√d, then passed through softmax so the weights for one query sum to 1. The output is the softmax-weighted sum of Values.

Why it matters here: a CNN only sees a local neighborhood. Attention lets a voxel on one side of the brain directly reference tissue on the other — essential for deciding whether two bright blobs belong to one tumor.

Multi-head attention — many questions at once

Instead of one attention map, the embed_dim is split into H heads of size d/H. Each head computes its own Q·Kᵀ·V in a different subspace, so one head can track "bright edges" while another tracks "spatial proximity". Outputs are concatenated and projected.

Swin: attention that doesn't explode in cost

Global attention over a 128³ volume is impossible — cost grows as (number of voxels)². Swin restricts attention to small windows (here, square tiles), making cost linear in volume. To let information cross window borders, alternating layers shift the window grid by half a window.

Window-only attention would trap each region in its own tile forever. The shift is what stitches the whole volume together over successive layers — global reach, local cost.

The U-Net shape — context down, detail up

Segmentation needs both global context (what is this?) and pixel precision (where exactly?). The U-Net solves this with a contracting encoder, an expanding decoder, and skip connections that hand high-resolution detail across the "U" so the decoder doesn't have to invent it.

OncoSeg's twist: the encoder is a Swin transformer, the decoder is CNN, and the skips are cross-attention (demo 09) instead of plain concatenation. Hover a stage to trace its skip. Channels 48→96→192→384 mirror back down.

Deep supervision — gradients reach every layer

Normally only the final output is scored. Deep supervision attaches a prediction head at each decoder stage and adds their losses (with decreasing weights), so even the deepest, coarsest layers get a direct training signal. This stabilizes training and helps tiny lesions survive downsampling.

Sliding-window inference — fitting a big scan through a small model

The model trains on 96–128³ ROI crops, but a real scan is 240×240×155. At test time a window of the ROI size slides across the volume with overlap; the overlapping predictions are averaged (Gaussian-weighted) so there are no seams at tile borders.

More overlap = smoother, slower. This is how OncoSeg processes a whole brain without ever holding it in memory at once. Raise the overlap and watch the seam artifacts vanish.

The output head — multi-label, not multi-class

Brain-tumor regions nest: ET ⊂ TC ⊂ WT. So OncoSeg does not use a 4-way softmax. It derives 3 overlapping binary regions — Tumor Core (labels 2,3), Whole Tumor (1,2,3), Enhancing (3) — and predicts each with an independent sigmoid. The channels need not sum to 1.

This is the BraTS convention and it changes everything downstream: the loss is BCE+Dice per channel, and uncertainty must use binary entropy (demo 18) — a subtle bug-source the review flagged. Toggle a voxel's true class to see which channels fire. train_all.py:71-74.

losses.py — Dice + BCE, and why Focal handles imbalance
Per-voxel loss vs predicted prob for a positive voxel. Focal down-weights easy cases.

The training objective — Dice + cross-entropy (+ Focal)

Tumor voxels are a tiny minority, so plain cross-entropy lets the model "win" by predicting all-background. OncoSeg combines Dice loss (optimizes overlap directly, scale-invariant) with BCE (per-voxel calibration). Focal loss adds a (1−p)^γ factor that shrinks the loss on easy, confident voxels so training focuses on the hard boundary.

Drag γ: at γ=0 Focal = BCE; as γ grows, confidently-correct voxels contribute almost nothing, so gradient budget flows to the ambiguous tumor rim. This is the class-imbalance fix at the heart of medical segmentation.

metrics.py — Dice overlap & why it punishes small lesions
Teal = ground truth. Amber = prediction. Overlap = where both agree. Drag prediction.

Dice: the overlap score that drives everything

Dice = 2·|A∩B| / (|A|+|B|) — twice the overlap over the total mask size. 1.0 = perfect, 0 = no overlap. It's the training loss and the headline metric because plain pixel accuracy is useless when 99% of voxels are background.

Shrink the prediction and watch Dice collapse: for a small tumor, missing a handful of voxels costs far more Dice than the same miss on a large one. This is precisely why OncoSeg's worst cases are small-TC tumors — and why the review flags ET (often empty) as producing NaN Dice.

Each pass randomly drops units → a slightly different mask. Spread across passes = uncertainty.

MC-Dropout: turning a network into an ensemble

Dropout normally only runs during training. Leave it on at inference and run the model N times: each pass silences a different random subset of units, giving N slightly different masks. The mean is the prediction; the per-voxel disagreement is the uncertainty.

Where passes agree (tumor center) → confident. Where they flicker (the boundary) → the model is honestly telling you "I'm not sure here" — the signal a radiologist would want highlighted.

Predictive entropy — quantifying "how unsure"

Once you have a probability, entropy turns it into a single uncertainty number. For a binary (sigmoid) output it's −p·log p − (1−p)·log(1−p) — peaking at p=0.5. The model is most uncertain at the decision boundary.

Review caveat made tangible: OncoSeg's output is sigmoid (independent channels), so binary entropy is correct. But inference.py uses the categorical formula −Σ p·log p, which omits the (1−p) mass and gives a different, invalid number. Drag p past 0.5 and compare the two curves.

Calibration & ECE — and how background voxels hide a problem

Calibration asks: when the model says "90% sure", is it right 90% of the time? Expected Calibration Error (ECE) averages the gap |confidence − accuracy| across confidence bins, weighted by how many voxels fall in each bin.

This is the project's most important finding. Toggle the background mass: when 98% of voxels are easy background, the count-weighted ECE looks tiny (~0.01, "well-calibrated") — even though on the foreground the model is badly over-confident (foreground ECE ≈ 0.49). The headline metric averages the problem away.

Hausdorff HD95 — Dice can't see boundary spikes

Dice measures volume overlap but is blind to shape: a prediction can have great Dice yet a single nasty spur far from the tumor. Hausdorff distance measures the worst boundary disagreement; HD95 uses the 95th percentile to ignore lone outlier voxels, reporting error in millimetres.

This is OncoSeg's secondary metric (15.4mm vs UNet3D's 21.0mm — its most defensible result). Add a boundary spike and watch HD95 jump while Dice barely moves — exactly why both metrics are reported together.

pretrain.py — DINO: emergent [CLS] attention, no labels
Patch grid = the input image. Amber dashed = the object. Teal = where the [CLS] token attends — diffuse early, locked onto the object late.

DINO: attention that finds the object — with zero labels

A Vision Transformer trained self-supervised (DINO: student matches a teacher across crops, no class labels) develops something striking: the [CLS] token's attention spontaneously localizes the salient object. Each patch gets an attention weight wi = softmax(β·featurei); we measure how peaked that map is with normalized entropy H = −Σ wi log wi / log N.

Drag training progress: at 0% features are random, so attention is uniform (H = 1.0) and the foreground gets only its fair area share (chance). As pretraining proceeds the inverse-temperature β grows, attention sharpens, entropy falls, and the foreground share races past chance toward ~100% — the object boundary emerges on its own.

Labels are the scarce resource in medical imaging — annotating a 3D tumor is hours of an expert's time. Self-supervised pretraining (DINO-style) is exactly how modern medical foundation models learn rich, object-aware features from oceans of unlabeled CT/MRI, so that fine-tuning a segmenter like OncoSeg needs only a handful of labeled scans. The entropy-drop / foreground-rise you see here is the signal that useful structure was learned before a single mask was ever shown.

Masked Autoencoder — learn by filling in the blanks

Cut the image into patches, hide most of them at random, and train the network to reconstruct the missing pixels from the few that remain. The encoder only ever sees the visible patches; a lightweight decoder paints the holes back in. No labels needed — the image is its own supervision.

The headline MAE finding (He et al., 2021): you can mask 75% of patches and still rebuild a plausible image, because natural scans are redundant — a handful of patches carry the gist. That redundancy is exactly why MAE-style self-supervised pretraining works so well for data-hungry 3D medical encoders: a model can learn anatomy from thousands of unlabelled volumes before ever seeing a hand-drawn tumor mask. Drag past 75% and watch the reconstruction stay structurally faithful — the tumor blob survives.

CLIP: matching a picture to the words that describe it

CLIP projects an image and each candidate caption into the same vector space, normalized to unit length. Their agreement is the cosine similarity cos(θ) = img·text — which, for unit vectors, is just the dot product. A temperature-scaled softmax over those cosines turns the scores into a probability per caption, and the highest one is the prediction.

This single idea is the engine of zero-shot classification: to label a scan you never trained a classifier on, you embed it once and compare it to text prompts like "an MRI of an enhancing glioma" vs "a normal brain MRI" — no new head, no fine-tuning. The same image↔text bridge is what lets vision-language models draft a radiology report from pixels. Drag the image embedding around the arc and watch the winning caption flip.

Left = noisy x_t (a brain slice + tumor blob). Right = clean target x₀. Amber arrows = the score: where the model pulls each pixel to remove noise.

Diffusion: corrupt with noise, then learn to walk back

A diffusion model defines a forward process that gradually drowns an image in Gaussian noise: x_t = √ᾱ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱ)·ε, where ᾱ(t) follows a cosine schedule from ≈1 (clean) at t=0 to ≈0 (pure noise) at t=1. A U-Net is trained to predict the noise ε at any t. Generation runs the process in reverse: estimate ε, subtract a step of it, repeat — climbing the score ∇ₓ log p(x_t) back onto the data manifold.

Diffusion is the SOTA engine for image synthesis and augmentation, and is now used as a segmentation prior: conditioning the reverse process can sample or sharpen plausible tumor masks, and the inherent stochasticity yields per-voxel uncertainty — the same signal the MC-Dropout widget estimates by a cruder route. Drag t: as noise rises the slice dissolves, σ grows, SNR drops, and the amber denoise arrows lengthen — that vector field is what the network learns to predict.

The whole-slide pyramid — when the image won't fit in memory

A digitized histology slide is a gigapixel image: scan a glass slide at 40× and you get on the order of 100,000 × 100,000 pixels ≈ 10¹⁰ pixels — about 30 GB raw. No GPU holds that. So the file is stored as a multi-resolution pyramid of fixed-size tiles (DeepZoom convention): level 0 is one coarse thumbnail tile, and each level up quadruples the resolution into 4ᴸ tiles (1, 4, 16, 64, 256, …) — higher level = finer detail = more tiles. You read only the tiles you need, at the zoom you need. (OpenSlide numbers it the other way: its level 0 is full-resolution.)

This is the opposite memory regime from OncoSeg. OncoSeg's MRI is a compact 128³ ≈ 2×10⁶-voxel volume that fits whole on one card — its challenge is 3D context. Histology can't fit at all, so the entire pipeline (tile → infer → stitch) is built around tiling, the same trick OncoSeg uses for inference (demo 13, sliding window) but forced on it from the very first byte.

H&E stain deconvolution — unmixing two dyes

In histology, Hematoxylin stains nuclei blue-purple and Eosin stains stroma/cytoplasm pink. The camera only sees their mixture. Each dye absorbs light along a fixed colour direction, so in optical-density space OD = −log₁₀(I/I₀) the two stains form a basis. Inverting that 2-vector basis recovers each dye's concentration per pixel — a linear unmixing, exactly Ruifrok & Johnston (2001).

Stain colour drifts between labs, scanners and batches — the histology analog of MRI intensity drift in demo 03. Deconvolving onto canonical H/E vectors (then renormalizing) is stain normalization: it makes inputs comparable before they hit the network, just like InstanceNorm. Spin the basis angle to mis-set the vectors and watch the nuclei "bleed" into the stroma channel — a wrong basis corrupts every downstream feature.

Multiple-Instance Learning — one label for a whole bag

A gigapixel slide is far too big to label per pixel, but a pathologist can say "this slide is cancer / not". MIL learns from exactly that: the slide is a bag of N patch instances, and only the bag carries a label. The model scores each patch, computes an attention weight aᵢ = softmax(tanh(V·hᵢ) ⊙ sigmoid(U·hᵢ)), and pools them into one prediction z = Σ aᵢ·pᵢ (Ilse et al. 2018, ABMIL).

The MI assumption: a bag is positive if any instance is positive. Drag the slider — with zero tumor patches the slide reads benign; the first tumor patch flips it to malignant and grabs most of the attention. This is how you'd pre-train OncoSeg's encoder on slides/scans labeled only "cancer/not" — no voxel masks — and the attention bars double as a free interpretable heatmap of which patches drove the call.

Pathology foundation models — morphology becomes geometry

A pathology FM (UNI, Virchow, …) is a huge encoder pretrained on millions of unlabeled tissue patches. It maps each patch to a feature vector in which patches that look alike land near each other. Tumor, stroma and immune patches form distinct clusters — so a brand-new patch can be classified just by looking at who its neighbors are.

This is the modern recipe: freeze the foundation encoder and train only a tiny head (here, a parameter-free kNN vote). It is exactly the OncoSeg move — start from a pretrained encoder rather than learning vision from scratch on a few hundred scans. Frozen features that cluster cleanly are the whole game; the head can be almost trivial.

ViT: the [CLS] token, and why position must be injected

A Vision Transformer cuts the image into patch tokens, prepends one extra learnable [CLS] token, and lets attention run. The [CLS] token attends to every patch and becomes a single global summary vector. But attention has no built-in sense of where a patch was — so each patch token gets a positional encoding added before the first layer.

Without PE, [CLS] pooling is a pure set operation: Σ softmax(q·tokenₛ)·valueₛ is permutation-invariant, so a scrambled image and the real one produce the identical output (drag PE to 0 and shuffle — ‖Δ‖ stays exactly 0.000). PE breaks that symmetry by binding a position signature to each slot. This is the encoder family behind SAM, DINO and the medical foundation models OncoSeg's Swin transformer descends from.

positional_encoding — sin/cos at geometric frequencies
Top: the 24-d position code for token p (teal=sin, amber=cos, per frequency). Bottom: PE(p)·PE(p+Δ) vs offset Δ — invariant to where p sits.

Positional encoding — telling a transformer where a token is

Self-attention is permutation-blind: shuffle the patch tokens and the math is unchanged. So before attention, each token at index p is stamped with a fixed vector built from sines and cosines at geometrically spaced frequencies ω_k = 10000^(−2k/d): PE[p,2k]=sin(p·ω_k), PE[p,2k+1]=cos(p·ω_k). Low-k pairs spin fast (fine local position); high-k pairs spin slowly (coarse global position) — like the hands of a set of clocks.

The magic property: PE(p)·PE(p+Δ) = Σ_k cos(ω_k·Δ) — it depends only on the relative offset Δ, never on the absolute p. That is what lets attention learn "attend to my neighbour" once and apply it everywhere. In OncoSeg this is how the Swin/transformer encoder knows a voxel-patch's location in the 3D grid without convolution's hard-wired locality — and it's the exact idea RoPE generalises by rotating Q/K instead of adding the code.

Left: gate scores the token against 8 experts. Center: top-2 light up (run); the rest stay idle. Bottom: per-expert load over a token stream.

Mixture-of-Experts — capacity without the compute bill

A dense layer runs every parameter on every token. A sparse MoE layer holds N expert sub-networks but a tiny gate picks only the top-k per token: logits = Wᵍ·x, then softmax, then keep the k largest and renormalize their weights so they sum to 1. The output is that gated blend of just the k chosen experts — the other N−k do zero compute.

This is how Mixtral 8×7B and DeepSeek-V3 hit frontier quality cheaply: 8 experts of parameters, but only 2 run per token → ~25% of the FLOPs of using all of them. For a 3D medical model the same trick lets you grow specialist capacity (one expert good at edemas, another at enhancing cores) without paying for all of it on every voxel patch. The catch — watch the bottom bars — is load imbalance: a skewed token stream sends most tokens to a few experts, so MoE training adds an auxiliary load-balancing loss to spread tokens out.

State-space models — linear-time sequence modeling

Self-attention compares every token to every other token, so its cost grows as N². A state-space model (Mamba) instead carries a single recurrent hidden state: hₜ = A·hₜ₋₁ + Bₜ·xₜ. It walks the sequence once, updating that state token-by-token — a selective scan — so cost grows only as N, with O(1) memory per step.

What makes it selective: the input gate Bₜ = σ(w·xₜ) is content-dependent. The model decides per-token how much of the new input to write into memory, and A sets how long old information lingers. Drag A toward 1 and the state remembers longer; lower it and memory decays fast.

For OncoSeg-style problems this matters whenever the sequence is long: a 128³ volume flattens to ~2 million tokens, and a longitudinal study stacks many timepoints. Quadratic attention is hopeless there, but a linear-time scan stays tractable — the same reason Swin windows (demo 08) cap attention cost. SSMs are the emerging alternative to making long 3D/temporal medical sequences affordable.

nerf.py — render one pixel by ray marching the volume integral
Top: the density field σ(x) with the marching ray (dashed) and its sample weights (coral dots) → the rendered pixel swatch. Bottom: per-sample σ·δ (teal bars), transmittance Tᵢ (coral, decaying) and the running accumulated opacity (dashed, rising).

NeRF: turning a 3D field into one pixel by marching a ray

A Neural Radiance Field stores a scene as a function that, for any 3D point, returns a density σ and a view-dependent color c. To render a single pixel you shoot a ray from the eye and take N samples along it. Each slab of length δ absorbs light with opacity αᵢ = 1 − e−σᵢδ. How much of that slab's color actually reaches the eye is gated by the transmittance Tᵢ = e−Σj σⱼδ = Πj(1−αⱼ) — the fraction of light that survived everything in front of it.

The final pixel is the transmittance-weighted integral C = Σ Tᵢ·αᵢ·cᵢ, and the accumulated opacity Σ Tᵢ·αᵢ = 1 − TN. Drag the slider to sweep the ray across the scene: a ray through empty space stays transparent (T barely decays, opacity ≈ 0); a ray through the dense teal core saturates early (T collapses, weights pile up at the first surface it hits, color locks onto that blob); a ray grazing the deeper coral satellite blends warm. The weights peak exactly at the first opaque surface — that is why NeRF reconstructs sharp geometry rather than a foggy average.

This is the same accumulate-along-a-ray operator that defines CT: a CT projection integrates attenuation Σ σᵢδ through tissue, and reconstruction inverts many such rays into a 3D volume. NeRF generalizes it — learning a continuous σ/c field from 2D views and rendering it differentiably — which is precisely the machinery behind learned 3D reconstruction of an organ or lesion from sparse 2D slices/projections, and behind novel-view synthesis for surgical and interventional planning. The transmittance term is also what makes the render occlusion-aware: a deep structure can't contribute color the moment something solid sits in front of it.

rasterizer.cu — project 3D gaussians → 2D, sort by depth, α-composite
Left = composited novel view (rasterized). Right = the depth-sorted splat stack; front splats (small depth) are blended first and occlude those behind.

3D Gaussian Splatting — render a scene by stamping fuzzy blobs

Instead of an implicit MLP, the scene is an explicit cloud of 3D gaussians, each with a position, an anisotropic covariance (its 3D shape), a color and an opacity. To synthesize a new view you project every gaussian to a 2D elliptical footprint, sort them front-to-back by depth, and alpha-composite them onto the image — exactly the "over" operator: C = Σᵢ cᵢ·αᵢ·Tᵢ with transmittance Tᵢ = Πⱼ₌₁ⁱ⁻¹(1−αⱼ). Each pixel's splat weight is αᵢ = opacityᵢ · exp(−½·dᵀΣ⁻¹d) — the projected gaussian evaluated at that pixel.

Drag splats rendered: the renderer adds gaussians in depth order. Early (front) splats lay down color and spend transmittance; once T drops near zero the surface is opaque and farther splats are culled — the same early-termination a real tile rasterizer uses.

The contrast with NeRF (demo on diffusion/JEPA-style implicit models) is the whole point: NeRF answers each pixel by marching a ray and evaluating a per-ray MLP at dozens of sample points — accurate but slow. Gaussian splatting is a single rasterize pass (sort + blend), no network in the loop, so it renders novel views at 100+ FPS. For medical/clinical use that means a 3D capture of a patient's anatomy or a surgical field can be re-viewed interactively in real time, and the explicit gaussians are directly editable (delete, recolor, deform a structure) in a way an opaque MLP volume is not.

Diffusion Policy — generate the plan by denoising it

Instead of emitting one action at a time, a diffusion policy treats the next H-step action trajectory as a single object to generate. It starts from a path of pure Gaussian noise and runs a learned reverse diffusion: at each step k (counting T→0) a network predicts the noise and subtracts a slice of it, so the random scribble collapses into a smooth, feasible path that reaches the goal and arcs around the obstacle — all conditioned on the observation.

Each waypoint follows the DDPM relation xₖ = √ᾱₖ·x₀ + √(1−ᾱₖ)·ε with a cosine schedule for ᾱ. Drag k from T down to 0: the signal weight √ᾱ rises, the noise level σ=√(1−ᾱ) falls, the signal-to-noise ratio climbs, and the trajectory snaps onto the clean target. The coral stubs are the denoise direction the model would apply next.

This is SOTA visuomotor control (Chi et al., 2023; π0). It is deliberately distinct from the VLA widget: VLA emits discrete, tokenized actions one bin at a time, whereas a diffusion policy produces a continuous, multimodal whole-trajectory distribution — switch the goal and it commits to a different smooth mode rather than averaging them. The same denoise-a-structured-output recipe is why diffusion is creeping into medical imaging as a segmentation / registration prior: sample plausible masks or deformations rather than regress one blurry mean — and for surgical-robot or auto-contouring deployment, generating a full feasible trajectory beats greedy per-step decoding.

Physics-Informed NN (PINN)

Fit a curve u(t) through a handful of noisy measurements of a damped harmonic oscillator. With only the data term, infinitely many wiggly curves pass near the points — the network happily picks one and overfits between samples. A PINN adds a second loss: the residual of the governing equation r(t) = u″ + 2ζω·u′ + ω²·u, which must be ~0 if u truly obeys the physics. Slide λ to weight that residual. At λ=0 the fit oscillates and r(t) is huge; as λ→1 the only curve that satisfies both the data and the equation is the smooth physical solution.

Total loss = Ldata + λ·Lresidual. The residual is evaluated everywhere via autodiff (here: finite differences), not just at labeled points — so the equation regularizes the gaps where labels are missing. In OncoSeg-style settings with scarce annotations, encoding known biology/physics (smooth boundary growth, conservation, dose kinetics) as a soft constraint stops the model from producing impossible predictions and improves generalization from tiny cohorts.

Quantization: FP32 -> INT8

A trained net stores each weight as a 32-bit float. Post-training quantization snaps those continuous values onto a small grid of integers. With a per-tensor scale s and symmetric mapping, each weight becomes w_q = round(w / s), an integer in [-2^(b-1), 2^(b-1)-1], and is read back as w_q · s. The choice s = max|w| / (2^(b-1)-1) spends the whole integer range on the observed weights. A b-bit code has only 2^b levels, so the grid spacing — and the rounding error — doubles for every bit you drop.

Why it matters for OncoSeg: a 3D segmentation backbone is large, but hospitals rarely have datacenter GPUs at the bedside. INT8 makes the weights 4× smaller (8/32 of FP32) and lets integer matmul run on a clinical workstation or edge appliance with near-identical Dice. Push too far, though — the accuracy strip shows INT8 and INT6 sit on a plateau, but at 2-bit the rounding noise swamps the signal and validation accuracy falls off a cliff. The engineering job is finding the lowest bit-width before that knee.

Knowledge distillation — train a small student on a big teacher's soft labels

A large teacher outputs logits over the classes. Instead of training a small student on the one-hot ground-truth label, distillation trains it to match the teacher's softened probabilities p = softmax(z / τ). A temperature τ > 1 flattens the distribution so the small probabilities the teacher assigns to the wrong classes become visible — and copyable.

Those small probabilities are "dark knowledge": the teacher quietly says this enhancing glioma also looks a bit like a meningioma or a post-op cavity, and nothing like a normal brain. The hard argmax keeps only the winner (entropy = 0); the soft target carries this whole similarity structure, so the student learns far more per example.

For deployment this is the compression lever: a 100M-parameter teacher can hand its behaviour to a student small enough to run on the scanner console or a single on-prem GPU — keeping accuracy while shrinking memory and latency at the point of care. It pairs naturally with pruning and quantization, and with self-supervised pretraining (DINO/MAE) which is how the teacher learned cheaply in the first place. The distillation loss is the KL between teacher and student soft targets, scaled by τ² to keep gradient magnitudes stable.

finetune.py — LoRA: freeze W, learn only ΔW = B·A

LoRA — fine-tune a giant model by training two thin matrices

A pretrained weight matrix W is d×d. Full fine-tuning re-trains all d² of its entries. LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) freezes W entirely and learns only a low-rank update ΔW = B·A, where B is d×r and A is r×d with rank r ≪ d. At inference the model uses W + B·A. The product of a (d×r) and an (r×d) matrix can have rank at most r, so ΔW is forced to be low-rank — and that is the whole point.

Drag rank r. Trainable parameters are 2·d·r instead of d²; the bar tracks how much of the ideal full update ΔW the rank-r factorization captures. Because real weight updates have a steeply decaying spectrum, even a tiny r recovers almost all of it — r=4 already captures >98% here.

This is the modern recipe for adapting a frozen medical foundation model to a new task on a single GPU. A 3D CT/MRI encoder may carry hundreds of millions of pretrained weights; retraining them for a new tumor type risks catastrophic forgetting and demands huge compute and data. LoRA instead bolts a handful of thin B/A factors onto each attention/projection layer — at d=4096 that is >99% fewer trainable parameters — so a hospital can specialize a shared foundation model to its own scanner and protocol from a small labeled set, ship just the megabytes-sized adapters, and keep the original weights untouched.

Mixed precision & loss scaling

Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) does the heavy matmuls in FP16 — half the memory, ~2× the throughput on tensor cores — while keeping an FP32 master copy of the weights for the actual update. The catch: FP16 can only represent magnitudes down to its smallest subnormal, 2-24 ≈ 6e-8. Any gradient smaller than that flushes to exactly 0 and that learning signal is lost forever.

Loss scaling rescues them. Multiply the loss by a large factor S (a power of two) before backward(); by the chain rule every gradient is scaled by the same S, shifting the whole cloud up into FP16's representable band. Right before the FP32 master-weight update you divide the gradients back by S — so the math is unchanged, you've only borrowed dynamic range. Drag the slider: watch the sub-floor gradients climb out of the underflow zone. Push S too far and the largest gradient hits the 65504 ceiling → Inf; a real dynamic loss scaler detects that, halves S and skips the step.

Per the OncoSeg training review, the notebook trained in pure FP32 and was the exact memory/throughput win it was missing. A 3D multi-scale tumor volume is enormous; AMP roughly halves activation memory, letting you fit a larger patch or batch (better BatchNorm/Dice statistics) and train faster on the same hospital GPU. Dice-style losses on tiny lesions produce very small gradients — precisely the regime where loss scaling prevents silent underflow that would otherwise stall learning on the smallest, most clinically important tumors.

Backprop: one rule, applied node by node

A tiny net: x → (·w) → ReLU → (·v) → L. Forward: z=w·x, h=max(0,z), y=v·h, loss L=½(y−t)². To learn, we need ∂L/∂w — but w is buried three operations deep. The chain rule threads it: ∂L/∂w = ∂L/∂y · ∂y/∂h · ∂h/∂z · ∂z/∂w = (y−t)·v·1[z>0]·x. Each edge carries its local derivative; backprop just multiplies them right→left, reusing the accumulated gradient at every step (that reuse is why it costs one backward pass, not one per weight).

Watch the ReLU gate: when z≤0 its local derivative ∂h/∂z = 1[z>0] = 0, so the incoming gradient ∂L/∂h hits a wall — ∂L/∂z = 0 and both ∂L/∂w and ∂L/∂x vanish. Slide x below 0 to kill the gradient. This is the literal mechanism behind "dead ReLUs," and it is exactly how every conv weight in OncoSeg's 3D U-Net is updated: forward a CT/MR volume, compute Dice/CE loss, backprop the same multiply-and-accumulate chain through hundreds of layers, step w ← w − η·∂L/∂w.

optim.py — SGD vs Momentum vs Adam on an ill-conditioned bowl
All three start at w₀ and roll toward the min. Coral=SGD, blue=Momentum, teal=Adam. The bowl is steep along w₁, shallow along w₂.

How the update rule shapes the path

Every optimizer descends the loss L=½(a·w₁²+b·w₂²) with gradient ∇L=(a·w₁, b·w₂), but they use it differently. SGD: w ← w − η·∇L — pure gradient, so along the steep axis it overshoots and zig-zags. Momentum: v ← β·v + ∇L, w ← w − η·v — a velocity that averages out the zig-zags and builds speed down the long valley (β=0.9). Adam: keeps running means of the gradient m̂ and its square v̂, then steps w ← w − η·m̂ ⊘ (√v̂ + ε) — dividing by √v̂ per parameter so each axis gets its own effective rate.

Drag η. The bowl is ill-conditioned (κ=a/b≈12): the largest stable SGD rate is set by the steep axis, η < 2/a. Push past η=2/a=0.125 and plain SGD oscillates and diverges — while Momentum and Adam stay stable. This is exactly why a 3D segmentation net like OncoSeg, whose loss curvature varies wildly across layers, is trained with Adam: it auto-scales the step for each weight so one bad direction can't blow up training.

Learning-rate schedule: warm up, then anneal

The step size η is not fixed. For the first t_w = w·T steps it ramps linearly from 0 to the peak η₀; after that it follows a cosine down to 0 over the remaining steps:

η(t) = η₀·t / t_w  for  t ≤ t_w
η(t) = ½η₀·(1 + cos(π·(t−t_w)/(T−t_w)))  for  t > t_w

Warmup keeps the first updates tiny while batch statistics and the optimizer's moment estimates (Adam's m̂, v̂) are still garbage — a full-size ∇L step on a fresh, badly-scaled net can explode the loss. Cosine decay starts fast to cover ground, then shrinks η smoothly so the final steps gently settle into a sharp, low-loss minimum instead of bouncing around it.

In medical-imaging segmentation a diverging first epoch wastes a long, expensive 3D training run. OncoSeg trained with a cosine schedule (with warmup) so the volumetric U-Net both started stably and annealed to a fine minimum — the same η(t) plotted here. Try warmup = 0 and watch the curve start at full η₀: that initial jolt is exactly what warmup is there to avoid.

Overfitting & early stopping

Train loss falls monotonically as the model keeps memorizing: L_train(t) = ε + (L₀−ε)·e^(−t/τ). But validation loss is the train term plus a variance term that grows as the model starts fitting noise: L_val(t) = L_train(t) + κ·(t/T)^β. That extra term makes val U-turn upward — the classic overfitting signature. The generalization gap is exactly that term: gap = L_val − L_train = κ·(t/T)^β, and it only widens.

The early-stopping rule: keep the checkpoint at the epoch where L_val is minimum (∂L_val/∂t = 0) and stop. Drag the epoch slider: before that point you are still learning signal; past it, train keeps dropping while val climbs — you are memorizing noise. The bottom panel shows the same story as a curve fit — low capacity underfits (high bias: misses the trend), high capacity overfits (high variance: chases every noisy point). Best test error sits in the middle, right where early stop lands.

This is the bias–variance tradeoff: E[err] = bias² + variance + σ²_noise. For OncoSeg, a 3D U-Net trained too long memorizes scanner texture and annotation jitter — train Dice keeps rising while held-out Dice falls. Early stopping on validation Dice (not the loss the README reports on train-time folds) is the cheapest regularizer; without a real held-out set, the "validated" numbers are just memorization. Watch the gap: a widening train↔val gap is the deployment red flag.

Data augmentation — one scan, infinitely many training views

Apply a label-preserving transform T to a training example and you get a brand-new, plausible scan for free. The catch in segmentation: a geometric T must hit the mask too — x̃ = T(x) and ỹ = T(y) — or the label no longer lines up with the pixels. Intensity transforms touch only x (the anatomy hasn't moved, so ỹ = y). In practice we sample destination voxels and read back through the inverse map x̃(p) = x(T⁻¹p), with bilinear interpolation, so image and mask warp by the identical field.

For small medical datasets this is essential, not optional. A site may have only dozens of annotated tumours; flips, ±40° rotations, scale, MR-intensity shifts and elastic deforms multiply the effective sample count and teach the network that a tumour is a tumour regardless of pose, zoom, or scanner brightness — i.e. it builds invariance and fights overfitting. OncoSeg trains on co-transformed 3D patches for exactly this reason.

Top: the layer stack, gradient flowing UP from the loss to layer 1. Bottom: |∇| at each depth — coral = plain, teal = with skips (log scale).

Residual connections — the identity highway for gradients

A plain deep net chains layers as x = F(x), so backprop multiplies one Jacobian per layer: the gradient reaching layer 1 is ‖∇₁‖ = Πᵢ ‖∂Fᵢ/∂x‖. If each factor is a typical ‖∂F/∂x‖ ≈ g < 1, that product is g·g·…·g = gD — it vanishes exponentially with depth, so the early layers stop learning. A residual block instead computes y = F(x) + x. Its Jacobian becomes ∂y/∂x = I + ∂F/∂x, and unrolling the whole stack (He et al. 2016) gives ∂L/∂x₁ = ∂L/∂x_L·(1 + ∂/∂x₁ Σᵢ Fᵢ). That standalone “1” is the identity skip path: it carries the deep gradient straight through to layer 1 with no attenuation, putting a hard floor of magnitude 1 on ‖∇₁‖. The full stack gradient is the product of per-block Jacobians ∏ᵢ(1 + ∂Fᵢ/∂x); with normalization keeping ‖∂F/∂x‖ ≈ g ≪ 1, each factor is ≈1, so the product stays ≈ const at every depth instead of vanishing.

This is why nets went from ~20 layers to 1000+. OncoSeg's 3D encoder is a stack of residual blocks, and every U-Net skip is the same idea at the feature-map level: the decoder adds back the encoder's high-resolution detail (concat/+) instead of re-deriving it through a long contracting path — so fine tumor-boundary gradients survive the bottleneck. Drag depth to ~50: the coral (plain) gradient at layer 1 falls past 10⁻¹³ — the early conv filters are effectively frozen and the rim never sharpens — while the teal (residual) gradient stays O(1) — never vanishing — no matter how deep the network gets.

Gaussian blur: the smoothing prior & scale space

Convolve the image with a Gaussian kernel G(x)=exp(−x²/2σ²), normalized so Σᵢ wᵢ = 1 (a partition of unity → a flat region stays flat, no brightening). One slider — σ — sets the scale. We truncate at radius r=⌈3σ⌉ (≈99.7% of the mass), so the kernel has 2r+1 taps. Each output pixel is a weighted average of its neighbourhood: x̂[n] = Σᵢ wᵢ·x[n−i].

The 2D Gaussian is separable: G(x,y)=G(x)·G(y), so a 2D blur = one horizontal 1D pass then one vertical 1D pass. That is O(2k) multiplies per pixel instead of O(k²) for the full square kernel — the speed-up grows linearly with kernel size. The mini-grid (coral) shows the outer product 1D ⊗ 1Dᵀ that the two passes reproduce exactly.

Larger σ = coarser scale: fine speckle noise vanishes first, then small structures, then edges soften — a continuous scale-space. This is the denoising/anti-alias prior under Difference-of-Gaussians, SIFT, edge detection, and the smoothing implicit in pooling. In OncoSeg-style pipelines a light Gaussian suppresses scanner noise before segmentation; but watch the small second lesion — push σ up and its contrast dissolves into the background, the same way over-smoothing erases the very small-TC tumors the model already struggles with. FWHM = 2√(2ln2)·σ ≈ 2.355σ reports how wide the blur really is in pixels.

Morphological ops — cleaning a segmentation mask

Slide a small structuring element B (a disk of radius r) over a binary mask A. The two atoms are: erosion A⊖B keeps a pixel only if B fits entirely inside A (every offset is foreground) — it shrinks objects and deletes specks; dilation A⊕B keeps a pixel if B touches A anywhere (any offset is foreground) — it grows objects and fills holes. In set terms: A⊖B = {z : B_z ⊆ A}, A⊕B = {z : B_z ∩ A ≠ ∅}.

Composing them gives the workhorses: opening A∘B = (A⊖B)⊕B — erode then dilate — removes objects smaller than B but keeps the survivors at their original size; closing A•B = (A⊕B)⊖B — dilate then erode — fills gaps and holes smaller than B without bloating the rest. Opening ≤ A ≤ closing always holds, and both are idempotent: (A∘B)∘B = A∘B.

This is the post-network clean-up step. A raw OncoSeg mask has flickering boundary voxels, isolated false-positive specks, and pin-holes inside solid tumor. Opening with a small disk erases the specks and thin bridges (so connected-components doesn't over-count lesions — recall the BRATS_077 case with 31 fragments); closing seals holes so a lesion is one solid object before its longest diameter is measured for RECIST. Pick the radius too large and opening will dissolve a genuine small tumor — exactly the small-TC failure mode the review flags.

Canny edge detection

Before learned segmentation, boundaries were found with a hand-built pipeline (Canny, 1986). First take the image gradient with the Sobel kernels and form the magnitude and orientation:  Gₓ = Kₓ ⋆ I, G_y = K_y ⋆ I, ‖∇I‖ = √(Gₓ² + G_y²), θ = atan2(G_y, Gₓ). A raw magnitude map gives fat, fuzzy ridges. Non-max suppression walks each pixel along its gradient direction θ (quantized to 0°/45°/90°/135°) and zeroes it unless it is the local maximum — collapsing every ridge to a clean 1-pixel line. Then a double threshold splits survivors into strong (‖∇I‖ ≥ Tₕ) and weak (Tₗ ≤ ‖∇I‖ < Tₕ), with the classic ratio Tₗ = 0.4·Tₕ. Finally hysteresis: keep a weak pixel only if it is 8-connected to a strong one (a flood-fill from the strong seeds). The slider is the high threshold Tₕ.

Slide it and watch the failure modes. Too low (Tₕ ≈ 0.05–0.10): texture and sensor noise survive — the final map explodes to 100–210 edge pixels in dozens of disconnected fragments (spurious "lesion" boundaries everywhere). Too high (Tₕ ≳ 0.30): the faint low-contrast disc never gets a strong seed, so hysteresis cannot rescue it and that whole object vanishes — a real but subtle boundary is missed. Only the mid band gives a clean, complete contour. This brittleness — one global threshold that is simultaneously too low somewhere and too high elsewhere — is exactly why faint, low-contrast tumor margins on CT/MRI defeat classical edge detectors, and why OncoSeg learns a data-driven, multi-scale boundary instead of thresholding a single gradient map.

Optical flow: reading motion from two frames

If a small patch just moves and keeps its brightness, then I(x,y,t) = I(x+u, y+v, t+1). A first-order Taylor expansion turns this brightness-constancy assumption into one linear equation per pixel: Iₓ·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0, where Iₓ,I_y are spatial gradients and I_t the frame-to-frame difference. We estimate the flow (u,v) that satisfies it. With a single equation and two unknowns the minimum-norm solution is the normal flow: (u,v) = −I_t·(Iₓ,I_y) / (Iₓ²+I_y²) — motion only along the gradient ∇I.

Aperture problem. One constraint can't pin down two unknowns, so locally we recover only the component of motion perpendicular to edges; sliding along an edge is invisible. Notice the arrows always snap toward the gradient direction and underestimate the true shift. In OncoSeg's temporal module this is why registering a tumor between baseline (t0) and follow-up (t1) scans is ill-posed in smooth interiors — usable motion lives at boundaries, and dense flow needs a smoothness prior (Horn–Schunck) or a window (Lucas–Kanade) to disambiguate.

IoU: the overlap ratio every detector is graded on

Intersection-over-Union (the Jaccard index) is IoU = |A∩B| / |A∪B| — the shared area divided by the area either box covers. For axis-aligned boxes the intersection is ∩ = max(0,Δx)·max(0,Δy) with overlap spans Δx, Δy, and the union is ∪ = |A| + |B| − ∩ (subtract once so the shared region isn't double-counted). IoU = 1 only for a perfect box; it falls to 0 the instant the boxes separate.

IoU is the single number that decides whether a predicted lesion counts: a detection is a true positive only if IoU ≥ τ (typically τ=0.5; COCO mAP sweeps 0.5→0.95). In OncoSeg this is how an automatically segmented tumor is matched to its ground-truth region before RECIST measurement — too loose and you over-count, too small or shifted and a real lesion is missed. Note IoU is stricter than Dice: for the same overlap, IoU = Dice/(2−Dice) ≤ Dice.

Non-max suppression: collapsing a swarm of boxes to one per lesion

A detector fires many overlapping candidate boxes per object, each with a confidence score. NMS is a greedy dedup: sort boxes by score, take the highest, then remove every other box whose overlap with it exceeds a threshold τ, and repeat on what remains. Overlap is the Intersection-over-Union
IoU(A,B) = |A∩B| / |A∪B|
i.e. shared area ÷ combined area, ∈ [0,1]. The survivor set is { keep i : score is max in its IoU>τ cluster }.

In OncoSeg this is the bridge from raw detections to RECIST target lesions: each true tumor should yield exactly one measured box, not five near-duplicates that would be summed five times. Lower τ suppresses more (aggressive dedup, risks merging two close lesions into one); higher τ keeps more (duplicates leak back, inflating the lesion count). Note the lone low-score box bottom-right never gets suppressed — nothing overlaps it; killing isolated false positives is a separate score-threshold step, not NMS's job.

Anchor boxes: turning detection into per-prior classify + regress

A detector can't emit arbitrary boxes, so it lays down a fixed grid of anchor priors — at every feature-map location it places K boxes spanning several scales and aspect ratios (here 3×3 = 9 per cell). Each anchor is then labelled by how well it overlaps the ground-truth object via Intersection-over-Union: IoU = |A∩B| / |A∪B|. Anchors with IoU > 0.5 become positive — the network must (a) classify them as "object" and (b) regress the residual offset Δ that snaps the prior onto the GT, encoded as tₓ=(gₓ−aₓ)/a_w, t_y=(g_y−a_y)/a_h, t_w=log(g_w/a_w), t_h=log(g_h/a_h). Anchors with IoU < 0.3 are negatives (background class); the band in between is ignored to avoid confusing labels.

Why multi-scale priors matter here: a single anchor size only matches objects of one size — drag the slider and watch positives vanish when the lesion is too small or too large for the current priors, then reappear as a different scale takes over. That is exactly why OncoSeg-style pipelines use multi-scale features: a region-proposal front-end with dense anchors first crops a tumour ROI at the right scale, then the 3D segmentation head refines it voxel-by-voxel. The log in the size targets makes the regressor learn scale-invariant ratios (so doubling a lesion's diameter is one fixed Δ, not a size-dependent one) — and unmatched anchors supplying the background class are what keep false positives down on a noisy scan.

Feature Pyramid Network — one detector, every scale

A backbone shrinks the image stage by stage, so deep maps are semantically strong but spatially coarse and shallow maps are fine but semantically weak. FPN keeps the whole stack as a pyramid P3..P7 (strides 8,16,32,64,128 → grids 32²,16²,8²,4²,2²) and adds top-down lateral connections: it upsamples a coarse map ×2 and adds it elementwise to the next finer lateral, Pₗ = up₂(Pₗ₊₁) ⊕ conv₁ₓ₁(Cₗ). Now every level is both high-resolution and semantically rich. An object is routed to the level whose anchor scale matches its size — the RetinaNet rule k = argminₖ |log₂(√(wh)/aₖ)|, anchors aₖ = {32,64,128,256,512} px. Small lesion → fine P3; bulky mass → coarse P7; either way the object spans only a handful of cells, which is exactly the scale the head was trained for.

Tumors arrive at wildly different scales — a sub-centimetre satellite nodule next to a 9 cm mass. A single-resolution feature map either blurs the small one into oblivion or starves the big one of context. FPN's top-down path is the detection cousin of U-Net skip connections: both carry coarse semantic context back into high-resolution maps so fine boundaries stay sharp. OncoSeg's encoder-decoder uses skips for voxel-accurate masks; a detector built on the same MRI features would use this pyramid to find lesions at any scale before segmenting them.

DETR: detection as set prediction

A transformer detector emits a fixed set of N predictions from N learnable object queries — each query outputs one box ŷᵢ = (class, cx, cy, w, h). Training needs to decide which prediction is responsible for which ground-truth object. DETR solves this with an optimal bipartite matching: find the permutation σ̂ minimising the total assignment cost
σ̂ = argminσ Σᵢ 𝓛match(yᵢ, ŷσ(i))
where the per-pair cost mixes class probability with box geometry
𝓛match = λL1·‖b−b̂‖₁ + λgiou·(1−GIoU) − p̂(class).

This minimum-cost assignment is computed by the Hungarian algorithm in O(N³) — a true one-to-one matching (every GT gets exactly one query, every query at most one GT). The N−M leftover queries are trained toward the no-object ∅ class. The set loss is then evaluated only on the matched pairs, so the network is directly penalised for emitting two boxes on one object — there is no NMS, no anchors.

Why it matters for OncoSeg-style detection: classic detectors fire many overlapping boxes per lesion and lean on NMS with a hand-tuned IoU threshold to dedupe — brittle for touching/satellite lesions and a hidden hyper-parameter at deployment. Set prediction makes duplicate suppression part of the loss: one query ↔ one lesion, end-to-end, threshold-free. The Radon transform — how a CT scanner sees

A CT scanner never photographs the inside of you directly. It fires X-rays straight through the body from one angle and a detector on the far side records, for each ray, how much was absorbed — the line integral of tissue attenuation μ along that ray. That 1-D readout is one projection. Mathematically this is the Radon transform: p(s,θ) = ∫ f(x,y) δ(x·cosθ + y·sinθ − s) dx dy, i.e. sum the slice f along every ray that hits detector position s when the gantry sits at angle θ. Rotate the gantry through θ ∈ [0°,180°), stack each projection as a column, and you get the sinogram p(s,θ) — a point source traces a sine curve through it, hence the name.

The sinogram is the measurement; the familiar cross-sectional image only appears after reconstruction (filtered back-projection or iterative/learned inverse — the adjoint operator fᵣₑc = ℛ⁻¹p). Two checks the toy honours exactly: (1) sweep θ and the mean ∫μ per ray stays constant (≈0.25 here) — projection conserves total attenuation, a true Radon invariant; only the shape of p(s,θ) changes as the elongated tumour rotates into/out of the beam. (2) Fewer angles → a sparser sinogram → a noisier reconstruction. This is the front door for OncoSeg's CT volumes: low-dose / sparse-view protocols (cancer patients are scanned repeatedly) leave streak artifacts in the reconstructed image that a downstream segmentation net must be robust to. Filtered backprojection: turning many X-ray views back into a slice

A CT scanner never measures the slice directly — it measures projections. Rotating around the patient, at each angle θ it records the line integral of attenuation across the body: the Radon transform p(s,θ) = ∫ f(x,y) dℓ along the line at offset s = x·cosθ + y·sinθ. Stack these over all θ and you get the sinogram. Reconstruction is the inverse problem: recover the image f from p.

The naive guess is backprojection: take each 1-D projection and smear it uniformly back across the image along its ray, then sum over angles — b(x,y) = ∫₀^π p(x·cosθ + y·sinθ, θ) dθ. This does place mass where the object is, but it also smears it everywhere else: every point bleeds out as a 1/r halo, so you get a hopelessly blurry star no matter how many angles you use (quality plateaus ~84% here).

The fix comes from the Fourier-slice theorem: the 1-D Fourier transform of a projection equals a radial slice of the 2-D transform of the image. Piling slices into a polar grid over-samples low frequencies by exactly 1/|ω|. So before backprojecting, convolve each projection with the ramp filter whose response is |ω| — the discrete Ram-Lak kernel h[0]=¼, h[odd]=−1/(π²n²), h[even]=0. The negative side-lobes sharpen each profile (you can see them dip below zero in the inset), cancelling the blur. The exact inversion is f = ∫₀^π (p *ₛ h)(x·cosθ + y·sinθ, θ) dθ. Toggle the ramp on and sweep the angle slider: few angles → streak artifacts; ramp + many angles → a crisp reconstruction (→97%).

This is not a toy analogy — it is the classical analytic CT reconstruction the field still ships, and the volumes that OncoSeg segments are themselves FBP (or its fan/cone-beam cousins) reconstructions. The tradeoffs shown here are the real upstream knobs on lesion conspicuity: fewer view angles = lower dose but streaks that can mimic or hide a small tumor; the ramp filter is what makes edges (and therefore lesion boundaries) crisp enough to segment. Iterative and deep-learning reconstructors are all benchmarked against this baseline, and sparse-view CT — pushing the angle count down to cut dose, then de-streaking with a network — is an active research frontier sitting directly on top of this picture.

k-space & Fourier: how an MR scanner draws a picture out of frequencies

An MRI scanner never measures pixels directly — it measures the spatial-frequency domain called k-space. Each acquired sample is one Fourier coefficient of the slice, and the image is recovered by the inverse 2-D Fourier transform: I(x,y) = Σkₓ Σk_y S(kₓ,k_y)·e+2πi(kₓx + k_y·y)/N, while the scanner fills k-space with the forward transform S(kₓ,k_y) = Σx Σy I(x,y)·e−2πi(kₓx + k_y·y)/N. The center of k-space is the low frequencies (the DC term S(0,0)=Σ I is total brightness) — it sets overall contrast and shape. The edges of k-space are high frequencies — they encode sharp edges and fine detail.

Drag the slider to set the fraction of k-space the scanner samples (a centered disk of radius R, area ∝ R²). Sampling only the bright center keeps contrast but the image goes blurry — every detail lives in the discarded high-frequency ring. Adding outer rings sharpens edges; the hard circular cutoff also injects faint Gibbs / truncation ringing (oscillations beside edges) — the same artifact you see in real undersampled scans. This is exactly the lever behind fast MRI: fewer k-space lines ⇒ shorter scan, at the cost of detail and aliasing, which compressed sensing then tries to recover.

OncoSeg ingests MR sequences (T1c, T2/FLAIR) as input channels — every one of those images is an inverse FFT of acquired k-space. Accelerated protocols undersample k-space to cut scan time and motion, so the network must stay robust to blurred lesion margins and ringing near boundaries — precisely where Dice and HD95 are most fragile. Understanding that the network sees a Fourier reconstruction (not raw tissue) clarifies why scanner/protocol shifts behave like a domain shift, and why low-frequency contrast can look fine while the high-frequency edges a segmentation depends on have been thrown away.

Image registration

Two scans of the same anatomy never land in the same coordinate frame — the patient shifts and rotates between acquisitions. Registration recovers a transform T that maps the moving image onto the fixed one, then resamples: warped(p) = M(R(θ)·p + t), with rotation R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ],[sin θ, cos θ]] and translation t = (dx, dy). We score the fit with an intensity similarity cost — here the sum of squared differences, SSD(T) = (1/N) Σᵢ (F(pᵢ) − M∘T(pᵢ))². Slide dx, dy, θ to drive SSD toward its single minimum: at the true transform the two images coincide and SSD → 0. That is exactly what an optimizer does — descend ∇T SSD until the basin bottoms out.

In OncoSeg this is the gate before any comparison. Mutual information — the multimodal alignment metric

To register a CT to an MRI you cannot subtract pixels: the same bone is bright in CT but dark on MR, so sum-of-squared-differences and correlation are meaningless across modalities. Information theory gives the fix. Treat the two overlapping images as random variables A (CT intensity) and B (MR intensity); their joint histogram p(a,b) records how often value a in one image coincides with value b in the other. Mutual information measures how much knowing one intensity tells you about the other: I(A;B) = ΣᵢΣⱼ p(a,b)·log₂[ p(a,b) / (p(a)·p(b)) ], equivalently I = H(A) + H(B) − H(A,B). When the slices align, each tissue's CT value maps consistently to one MR value, so the joint histogram collapses onto a few tight clusters, joint entropy H(A,B) drops, and I(A;B) is maximal. Misalign them and pixels from different tissues get paired at random — the histogram smears, H(A,B) rises, and I(A;B) falls. Registration is therefore just: find the transform T that maximizes I(A;B).

Drag Δx to misregister, and watch the joint histogram blur while I(A;B) slides off its peak; return to Δx=0 and it snaps back to a sharp maximum. The decisive point: MI peaks at alignment regardless of the intensity relationship — here the CT↔MR map is even inverted for bone, so the Pearson correlation at perfect alignment is only r≈0.24 and SSD/correlation would never find the answer, yet MI does. This is exactly why MI is the default similarity metric for multimodal registration (ITK/Elastix/ANTs Mattes-MI). In OncoSeg's response pipeline, baseline and follow-up scans from different scanners or sequences are co-registered by maximizing MI before tumor volumes are compared — without that alignment, a longitudinal ΔV is just noise.

GAN: a two-player minimax game

Two networks fight. The generator G maps noise z→x̂ trying to fake the real data; the discriminator D outputs D(x)=P(real) and tries to catch fakes. They optimize minG maxD Ex∼pdata[log D(x)] + Ez[log(1−D(G(z)))]. For a fixed G the best critic is the optimal discriminator D*(x) = pdata(x) / (pdata(x) + pg(x)). Plug it back in and G is minimizing 2·JSD(pdata ‖ pg) − log4. The unique global optimum is pg = pdata, where D*(x) = ½ everywhere (the critic is reduced to a coin flip) and JSD = 0. Drag the training step and watch the coral cloud slide onto the teal one while the blue D-curve flattens to the dashed ½ line.

Why it matters for OncoSeg-style imaging: labeled tumor volumes are scarce and scanners disagree. GANs synthesize realistic lesions to augment tiny training sets, harmonize CT/MRI appearance across sites (CycleGAN-style domain transfer), and the adversarial loss sharpens segmentation/translation outputs. The same D≈½, JSD→0 signature is your convergence check — if D stays confident (D(real)≫D(fake)), the generator is still losing and the synthetic scans are detectable artifacts, not training-worthy data. Mode collapse (the coral cloud covering only one real peak) is the classic failure to watch for.

Variational Autoencoder — a latent space you can sample from

An autoencoder squeezes x through a bottleneck and rebuilds it. A VAE makes that bottleneck probabilistic: the encoder emits not a point but a Gaussian q(z|x)=N(μ,σ). We draw a code with the reparameterization trick z = μ + σ⊙ε,  ε∼N(0,1) — the randomness lives in ε, so gradients still flow through μ and σ. The decoder turns z back into x̂. Training minimizes L = ‖x−x̂‖² + β·KL(N(μ,σ)‖N(0,1)): a reconstruction term plus a pull dragging every posterior toward the unit Gaussian prior, with KL in closed form KL = ½Σₖ(μₖ² + σₖ² − 1 − ln σₖ²).

That KL term is the whole point. It forces neighbouring codes to overlap the prior, so the latent space becomes smooth and continuous — you can then sample z∼N(0,1) and decode plausible new images, or interpolate between two scans and watch anatomy morph cleanly. The β knob trades the two goals: β→0 collapses σ→0 (a deterministic autoencoder) → razor-sharp recon but a punctured, ungeneratable latent; large β drives σ→1, μ→0 → a beautifully regular, sampleable manifold but a blurry x̂. In OncoSeg this is how you build a generative tumor-shape prior: learn the manifold of normal/abnormal anatomy, sample synthetic lesions for augmentation, or flag out-of-distribution scans by their reconstruction error. (The μ/σ here follow the exact ELBO optimum μₖ*=2mₖ/(2+β), σ²*=β/(2+β) for a toy Gaussian model — drag β and the recon↑ / KL↓ tradeoff is the real one.)

Flow matching / rectified flow — generate by transporting noise along straight paths

Diffusion learns to denoise over dozens of stochastic steps. Flow matching instead learns a deterministic velocity field v(x,t) that transports a sample as time runs t: 0 → 1, governed by the ODE dx/dt = v(x,t). We pick the simplest possible bridge between a noise sample x₀ and a data sample x₁ — a straight line: xₜ = (1−t)·x₀ + t·x₁, whose conditional velocity is the constant u = x₁ − x₀. The network is trained by regression (no likelihoods, no noise schedule): min E‖v(xₜ,t) − (x₁−x₀)‖². The field it learns is the marginal velocity v(x,t) = E[ x₁ − x₀ | xₜ = x ]. At sample time we just integrate it forward with explicit Euler steps: x ← x + v(x,tₖ)·Δt, Δt = 1/steps.

This widget computes the exact marginal velocity in closed form (Gaussian noise → a weighted mixture of data modes), so the physics is real, not faked. Drag steps: with just 4–8 Euler steps the coral noise cloud already lands cleanly on the teal data modes — where diffusion needs dozens of denoising steps. That speed is the whole point: rectified flow (Liu 2022) and conditional flow matching (Lipman 2023) are the SOTA successors to diffusion and power Stable Diffusion 3, Flux and SiT. Because the paths are nearly straight (path-length ÷ chord ≈ 1), few-step integration barely strays from the true ODE solution. The catch lives at steps = 1: a single step lands at the prior mean E[x₁] — the honest failure mode that motivates more steps or reflow. For OncoSeg this is the fast generative engine: synthesize realistic lesion appearances for augmentation, or sample plausible tumor masks as a segmentation prior, in a handful of forward passes — cheap enough to actually deploy.

ROC & AUC: scoring a classifier without committing to a threshold

Two rates fall out: TPR = TP/(TP+FN) (sensitivity — caught positives) and FPR = FP/(FP+TN) (false alarms = 1−specificity). Slide t and the operating point (FPR, TPR) traces the ROC curve. Move t: the dot slides along the curve, trading sensitivity for specificity, but the curve — and the AUC — never change. That is the point: AUC judges how well scores separate the classes, independent of where a clinician later sets the alarm. In OncoSeg a tumor/normal voxel head with AUC 0.5 is coin-flipping (diagonal); 1.0 perfectly ranks every positive above every negative. Precision, recall & F1 — the right metric for rare tumour voxels

Move the decision threshold and a single classifier becomes many. Of the voxels it calls tumour, what fraction really is? — P = TP / (TP + FP). Of the voxels that truly are tumour, what fraction did it catch? — R = TP / (TP + FN). Raising the threshold trades recall away for precision; lowering it does the reverse. Their harmonic mean balances both: F1 = 2·P·R / (P + R), maximal only when neither is sacrificed.

The plot sweeps the threshold across the same two score distributions — abundant negatives, a 2% sliver of positives — and traces precision against recall. Drag the slider to ride the curve: at threshold 0 you flag everything (R = 1) but precision collapses to the prevalence (0.02); at threshold 1 precision → 1 but recall → 0. The coral dot is your operating point, the teal tick marks the F1 peak.

Under heavy imbalance, ROC lies by omission. A 2%-tumour scan gives AUROC ≈ 0.89 (looks excellent) while AUPRC ≈ 0.26 — because the huge true-negative mass makes the false-positive rate tiny even when false positives outnumber true positives. The PR curve's baseline is the prevalence, not 0.5, so it shows the real cost of crying "tumour" on healthy tissue. For rare-class segmentation like OncoSeg, F1 / Dice and the PR curve — not accuracy or ROC — are the honest scoreboard, and the operating threshold is tuned to the F1 peak (or the clinical precision/recall the use-case demands).

Confusion matrix: where the model mistakes which class

A 4-class tissue classifier (Background, Edema, Tumor Core, Enhancing) is scored by counting, for every true class i, how its Nᵢ voxels were predicted. Cell Mᵢⱼ = #(true=i, pred=j). The diagonal Mᵢᵢ is correct; everything off it is an error. Two complementary scores read it in orthogonal directions: recall reads a row — Rᵢ = Mᵢᵢ / Σⱼ Mᵢⱼ (of the real i's, how many did we catch?) — while precision reads a column — Pⱼ = Mⱼⱼ / Σᵢ Mᵢⱼ (of everything we called j, how many were truly j?). Drop accuracy and watch each row's mass leak off the diagonal — counts don't vanish, they migrate into the cells the model confuses. Notice the asymmetry — Background's huge support keeps its precision high even as recall falls, while the small Enhancing class loses both first. That is why OncoSeg reports per-region Dice/recall, never a single accuracy: a 90%-accurate model can still miss most of the enhancing tumor, and only the confusion matrix exposes which class it confuses for which.

Bootstrap confidence interval — a number without error bars is a guess

You evaluate on n test subjects and report mean Dice = 0.81. But that single number is itself a random draw — run on a different 16 patients and you'd get something else. The nonparametric bootstrap (Efron, 1979) estimates that sampling spread using only the data in hand: draw a resample of size n with replacement from the scores, recompute the statistic θ̂*ᵦ = (1/n)·Σᵢ xᵢ*, repeat B times, then read the empirical 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the {θ̂*₁…θ̂*ᵦ} as the 95% CI. The spread of that bootstrap cloud is the standard error: SE ≈ √(Σ(θ̂*ᵦ − θ̄*)² / B), which here tracks the textbook s/√n.

This is the direct fix for the review's single-run, no-CI problem. OncoSeg headlines a point Dice and an ECE = 0.0101 computed from one subject and 5 MC samples, then claims significance — with no interval to say whether the gap survives sampling noise. Drag B: at B≈10 the CI jumps around wildly (Monte-Carlo noise ∝ 1/√B); by B≈1000–2000 it locks in at width ≈ 0.14. Note the percentile CI is asymmetric here — the low-Dice failure cases pull the left tail out — which a symmetric ±1.96·SE bar would hide. The cost is trivial: report 0.81 [0.74, 0.88] and the reader can judge the claim.

k-fold cross-validation: an error bar for your score

Shuffle the data, cut it into k equal folds, then run k rounds: round i trains on the other k−1 folds and validates on fold i. Every sample is validated exactly once. The reported estimate is the average of the k held-out scores, with a spread: CV = (1/k)·Σᵢ sᵢ and σ = √( (1/(k−1))·Σᵢ (sᵢ − CV)² ). The number you publish is CV ± σ, not a single lucky split.

On a single train/val split, a 200-patient oncology cohort can give a Dice that swings several points just from which patients landed in val. k-fold spends the whole dataset on evaluation: each patient is held out once, so the mean is a far more honest estimate and σ tells you how split-sensitive the model is. Crank k up and the folds get smaller, so each per-fold score gets noisier and σ across folds actually grows — k=5 or 10 is the standard bias/variance/compute sweet spot, not the σ-minimizer. That is why small medical datasets (like OncoSeg's tumor cohorts) report 5-fold CV rather than one split, and why a model-comparison claim without an error bar is not yet trustworthy.

A CNN's last conv block emits K feature maps Aₖ(i,j) — each a spatial detector for some learned pattern (a lesion core, an organ, a bright rim). That is the gradient flowing back into the map, averaged over space: αₖ = (1/Z) Σᵢⱼ ∂yᶜ/∂Aₖ(i,j) with Z = H·W. For this GAP+linear head that average is exactly αₖ = wᶜₖ/Z. Integrated gradients — attribution that obeys an axiom

A raw saliency map ∂F/∂x is just the gradient at the input. But neurons saturate: once a feature is strong, its local gradient flattens to ~0, so the pixel that caused the prediction can get zero credit. Integrated Gradients fixes this by walking a straight line from a neutral baseline x' (a black image) to the input x and averaging the gradient all along the way:

IGᵢ = (xᵢ − x'ᵢ) · ∫₀¹ ∂F/∂xᵢ |x'+α(x−x') dα

The slider sets N, the number of Riemann samples approximating that integral: IGᵢ ≈ (xᵢ−x'ᵢ)·(1/N)·Σk=1..N ∂F/∂xᵢ|α=k/N. More steps ⇒ a finer sum ⇒ a sharper, more faithful map.

The killer property is completeness: summing the attributions returns the model's score gain exactly — Σᵢ IGᵢ = F(x) − F(x'). With few steps the coarse sum undershoots (watch the coral bar fall short of the teal target and the gap shrink ~1/N as you slide right). For OncoSeg this is the difference between a heatmap a radiologist can trust — every unit of predicted tumor likelihood is accounted for by specific voxels — and a pretty-but-unverifiable saliency picture. IG needs only gradients (one backward pass per step), no architecture changes, which is why it is a default for auditing medical-imaging models.

Attention rollout: what a ViT is really looking at

A single layer's attention map A is misleading — every layer also passes information straight through its residual connection. Rollout corrects this: per layer it forms Ã = ½A + ½I (half attention, half identity), then composes layers by matrix multiplication: R = ÃL · ÃL−1 ⋯ Ã1. Because each Ã is row-stochastic (rows sum to 1), so is R — the [CLS] row R[CLS, ·] is a genuine probability distribution over input patches: "how much does each patch flow into the output token."

Watch the contrast: raw last-layer CLS attention smears across the image (≈30% on the tumor), but as you compose layers the rollout concentrates onto the object (≈66%). This is exactly how ViT/DINO/VLM saliency maps are made — and why a clinician-facing OncoSeg explanation should use rollout, not a single attention head, to claim "the model focused here." PCA: rotate to the axes of maximum variance

Center the data, form the covariance Σ = (1/n)·Σᵢ (xᵢ−x̄)(xᵢ−x̄)ᵀ, then eigendecompose it: Σ vₖ = λₖ vₖ. The eigenvectors v₁,v₂ are the principal axes (orthogonal, sorted by λ); each eigenvalue λₖ is exactly the variance of the data measured along that axis. PC₁ is the single direction that captures the most spread — projecting onto it, z = v₁ᵀ(x−x̄), is the best 1-D summary of a 2-D point. Fraction kept = λ₁/(λ₁+λ₂); the discarded coral drop-lines are the reconstruction error PC₂ would have stored.

Rotate the cloud and watch the axes spin to follow it while λ₁, λ₂ and %-variance stay frozen — PCA is rotation-invariant, it finds intrinsic structure, not the coordinate frame you happened to use. Raise the aspect (make the blob round) and PC₁'s share collapses toward 50%: no preferred direction left to compress. This is the engine behind decorrelation and linear dimensionality reduction — the same trick that turns thousands of OncoSeg radiomic / deep features per lesion into a handful of variance-ranked components for visualization, denoising, and batch-effect removal before downstream response modeling.

t-SNE / UMAP — neighborhoods, not distances

A deep encoder turns each image (or patch) into a high-D feature vector. To see that space we squash it to 2-D — but PCA only keeps global variance and smears overlapping clusters. t-SNE/UMAP instead preserve who is near whom. For each point t-SNE fits a Gaussian whose width σᵢ is tuned so its neighbor distribution hits a target perplexity: pⱼ|ᵢ = exp(−‖xᵢ−xⱼ‖²/2σᵢ²) / Σₖ exp(·). In 2-D it uses a heavy-tailed Student-t kernel qᵢⱼ ∝ (1+‖yᵢ−yⱼ‖²)⁻¹ and minimizes KL(P‖Q) = Σᵢⱼ pᵢⱼ·log(pᵢⱼ/qᵢⱼ) by gradient descent. Perplexity ≈ the effective number of neighbors each point listens to.

This is how you sanity-check a frozen pathology foundation model (UNI, Virchow) or any OncoSeg encoder: embed thousands of patches, run t-SNE/UMAP, and look for cleanly separated clusters by tissue type — that means the features are linearly probe-able and a tiny head will work. Two traps to teach: (1) perplexity too small → clusters shatter into specks (you "see" structure that is just noise); too large (→ N) → distinct clusters merge. (2) The gap size between clusters and a cluster's overall position carry no metric meaning — never read "tumor is far from stroma, close to immune" off a t-SNE plot. Use it to spot batch effects and mislabels, not to measure distances.

k-means clustering — unsupervised grouping by similarity

Given points {xᵢ} and a chosen k, k-means seeks centroids {μⱼ} minimising the within-cluster scatter J = Σᵢ ‖xᵢ − μc(i)‖² (the inertia), where c(i)=argminⱼ‖xᵢ−μⱼ‖. There is no closed form, so Lloyd's algorithm alternates two cheap steps until labels stop changing: (1) assign every point to its nearest centroid, then (2) update each centroid to the mean of the points assigned to it, μⱼ = (1/|Cⱼ|) Σi∈Cⱼ xᵢ. Each step can only lower (or hold) J — assignment minimises J for fixed μ, the mean minimises J for fixed labels — so the curve on the right is monotonically non-increasing and the method converges to a local optimum. Drag the iteration slider to watch points recolour and centroids slide from their seeded ring toward the blob centres.

This is the workhorse of unsupervised medical-imaging analysis: cluster voxels in a (T1, T2, FLAIR) intensity space to separate tissue types — CSF, grey/white matter, tumour core vs oedema — with no labels at all, a fast first pass before a supervised model like OncoSeg refines the boundary. Two caveats the widget makes visible: J always drops, so a lower J does not mean a better clustering — push k up and J keeps falling even past the 3 true blobs (over-segmentation); and the result depends on initialisation. Set k=3 (matching the data) for the cleanest, lowest-J solution.

InfoNCE: learning by comparison, with no labels

Take one image, make two random augmentations (crop, flip, jitter) — that is a positive pair. Every other image in the batch is a negative. An encoder maps each view to an L2-normalized embedding, so similarity is pure cosine: sim(u,v) = u·v. The InfoNCE loss is a softmax cross-entropy that treats "which of these is my positive?" as an N+1-way classification: L = −log [ exp(sim⁺/τ) / ( exp(sim⁺/τ) + Σⱼ exp(simⱼ⁻/τ) ) ]. Minimizing L pulls z and z⁺ together (numerator up) while pushing every z⁻ away (denominator down).

This is the engine behind SimCLR / MoCo and the self-supervised pretraining of modern foundation models — including medical-imaging encoders. With unlabeled MRI/CT/WSI, two augmented views of the same scan must agree while different patients are repelled, so the network discovers tissue, anatomy and texture structure before any radiologist label exists. A few annotated scans then fine-tune a tiny head — exactly how OncoSeg-style backbones get data-efficient. Temperature τ is the key knob: small τ sharpens the contrast (heavily penalizing hard negatives), large τ softens it (a hedged, lower-resolution comparison). Drag the training step and watch L collapse as the geometry sorts itself out.

Policy gradient: reward-weighted imitation of your own actions

A policy is a distribution over actions. Here it is a 1-D Gaussian πθ(a)=N(μ,σ²) with parameters θ=(μ, log σ). We want to maximize the expected reward J(θ)=𝔼a~πθ[R(a)]. The policy-gradient theorem gives a gradient we can actually estimate from samples: ∇θ J = 𝔼[∇θ log πθ(a)·R(a)]. In words: sample an action, see its reward, then nudge θ to make high-reward actions more probable and low-reward actions less probable — reward-weighted imitation of your own behavior.

For a Gaussian policy the score function is exact: ∂ log π/∂μ = (a−μ)/σ² and ∂ log π/∂(log σ) = (a−μ)²/σ² − 1. Drag training step: the mean μ climbs toward the high-reward action a*, and once reward concentrates near the mean the variance term goes negative so σ shrinks — the policy stops exploring and commits. The bottom curve is the true J=𝔼[R], rising from ≈0.08 to ≈0.94.

This is the seed of modern alignment and control. RLHF and its successors (PPO, GRPO) optimize a language policy with exactly this reward-weighted log-prob gradient, where R is a learned human-preference reward — it is how Claude-style assistants are tuned. The same update trains continuous robot policies. We subtract a baseline b=𝔼[R] (variance reduction, still unbiased: 𝔼[∇log π·b]=0) and use the exact expected gradient via Gauss–Hermite quadrature, so the trajectory is deterministic and the learning curve is reproducible — the discipline you'd want before letting a reward-tuned agent touch a clinical workflow.

Value iteration on a gridworld

An agent in a grid wants to reach the GOAL (reward +1) and avoid the PIT (−1). It does not know the route in advance — it learns a value for every cell: V(s) = the expected discounted return from s if it acts optimally. Each sweep applies the Bellman optimality update V(s) ← maxₐ[ r(s,a) + γ·V(s′) ], where the action-value is Q(s,a) = r + γ·V(s′) and the discount γ=0.90 makes far-off reward worth less. Start with V=0 everywhere. On sweep 1 only cells next to the GOAL learn a value; on each later sweep the value propagates one ring backward, so the teal heat fills outward from the goal. Slide the iteration count and watch the values flood the grid and the greedy arrows ↑↓←→ snap into a coherent path. At convergence V(start) ≈ γ¹⁰ = 0.349 — exactly the discounted +1, ten steps away.

This is the backbone of value-based RL (Q-learning is the same update, but sampled from experience instead of swept over the known model). The lesson generalizes far past toy grids: reward propagates backward from outcomes, and the greedy argmax over learned values is the policy. In a clinical decision pipeline downstream of OncoSeg, you could frame treatment-response follow-up scheduling the same way — states = a patient's current tumor-burden / response status from the segmenter, actions = scan-now vs. wait vs. escalate, reward = catching progression early minus the cost/dose of imaging. Value iteration then yields a policy that looks ahead: it tolerates a small near-term cost (an extra scan) because the discounted future value of catching progression early is high — the same "route around the pit, accept a longer path to the goal" reasoning you see filling the grid here.

Recurrence and the vanishing gradient — why gating mattered

A recurrent net reads a sequence one step at a time, folding each input into a single carried hidden state: hₜ = tanh(Wₓ·xₜ + Wₕ·hₜ₋₁). To learn that something early in the sequence matters, the gradient must flow back through every step. One step back the Jacobian is ∂hₜ/∂hₜ₋₁ = Wₕ·tanh′(zₜ) = Wₕ·(1−hₜ²), so the signal carried across t steps is the product ∏ Wₕ·tanh′ ≈ Wₕᵗ. Since tanh′ ≤ 1 and usually |Wₕ|·tanh′ < 1, that product shrinks geometrically — the vanishing gradient. Drag the distance slider: the coral curve collapses to ~0 within a handful of steps.

The LSTM fixes this with a separate cell state and a forget gate: cₜ = fₜ ⊙ cₜ₋₁ + iₜ ⊙ c̃ₜ, with fₜ = σ(Wf·[hₜ₋₁,xₜ]). Now the back-prop path through the cell is just ∂cₜ/∂cₜ₋₁ = fₜ, so signal over distance t is ∏ fₜ = fᵗ — an additive, near-identity highway (the constant-error carousel). Push f → 1 and the teal curve stays flat: information rides across hundreds of steps. The gate chooses what to keep instead of mangling it through a saturating tanh.

This is the sequence model that ruled before transformers, and the lesson generalizes to OncoSeg. A longitudinal study stacks many timepoints (baseline, follow-up, …) and a tumor's behavior at scan 1 must still inform the response call at scan 6; a naive recurrence would forget it. Gating — keep an additive memory path whose per-step multiplier is ≈1 — is the same trick that residual/skip connections use in the U-Net (demo 11) and that self-attention later replaced entirely by letting any step read any other directly (∝N²). Knowing why gating was needed is why attention was the breakthrough it was.
